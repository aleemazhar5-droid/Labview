## What Is Space Weather?

**Space weather** describes the conditions in space — on the Sun, in the solar wind, and in the Earth's magnetosphere, ionosphere and thermosphere — that can affect technology and human life. Just as terrestrial weather has its storms, space weather has its eruptions. There are **solar flares** that flood the sunlit side of the Earth with X-rays in minutes, and **solar energetic particle (SEP) events** that fill near-Earth space with high-energy protons. There are also **coronal mass ejections (CMEs)** — billion-tonne clouds of magnetised plasma that can trigger **geomagnetic storms** when they reach the Earth one to four days later.

Modern society is increasingly exposed. Satellites can be damaged or lost, and GNSS positioning degrades. High-frequency radio used by aviation and maritime users blacks out, and airline passengers and crew receive extra radiation on polar routes. Long power lines and pipelines carry damaging induced currents. Several governments, including those of the UK and the USA, list severe space weather among their national risks. Insurance studies have estimated that a storm comparable to the largest on record could cause economic losses of hundreds of billions to trillions of dollars. These losses would come mainly through prolonged power outages.

## The Sun: A Variable Star

### Structure

The Sun is a sphere of hot plasma 1.39 million km across, powered by nuclear fusion in its core at about 15 million K (Chapter 2). Energy travels outward by radiation through the **radiative zone** and then by convection through the **convection zone**, the outer 30 % of the radius. The visible surface, the **photosphere**, has a temperature of about 5 800 K. Above it lie the thin **chromosphere** and the tenuous **corona**, which is mysteriously heated to 1–2 million K and continuously expands into space as the **solar wind**. NASA's **Parker Solar Probe**, which on 24 December 2024 passed just 6.1 million km from the Sun's surface at a record 192 km/s, is investigating how the corona is heated and the wind accelerated.

### Magnetic Activity and the Solar Cycle

The Sun's activity is driven by its **magnetic field**, generated by a dynamo. In this dynamo, differential rotation (the equator rotates in about 25 days, the poles in about 35) and convection stretch, twist and amplify the field. Where bundles of strong magnetic field emerge through the photosphere, they inhibit convection and create cooler, darker **sunspots** (@fig:sunspot), surrounded by active regions where flares and CMEs originate.

The number of sunspots rises and falls in a cycle of about **11 years** (@fig:cycles), during which the Sun's global magnetic field reverses; the full magnetic cycle therefore lasts about 22 years. Systematic sunspot counts began in the 18th century; the cycle that began in 1755 is numbered Cycle 1. Cycles vary greatly in strength: Cycle 19, peaking in 1958, was the strongest recorded, while from about 1645 to 1715 — the **Maunder Minimum** — sunspots almost disappeared.

![The sunspot number since 1749. Cycle numbers are shown at the top. The strength and length of cycles vary considerably.](figures/out/ch22_cycles.svg){#fig:cycles width=100%}

**Solar Cycle 25** began in December 2019. A NOAA–NASA expert panel predicted a weak cycle peaking at a smoothed sunspot number of about 115, similar to Cycle 24. Instead, activity rose faster and higher, reaching a smoothed maximum of about 161 in October 2024 (@fig:cycle25). The declining phase, now under way, is historically when many of the largest geomagnetic storms occur, driven both by CMEs and by high-speed solar-wind streams from coronal holes.

## Solar Eruptions and the Solar Wind

### Solar Flares

A **solar flare** is a sudden release of magnetic energy in an active region — up to about 10²⁵ J in minutes to hours. It heats plasma to tens of millions of kelvin and accelerates particles. Flares emit across the spectrum from radio waves to gamma rays; their X-ray and extreme-ultraviolet radiation reaches the Earth in about 8 minutes and immediately increases ionisation in the dayside ionosphere. Flares are classified by their peak soft X-ray flux at 0.1–0.8 nm, measured by GOES satellites. The classes are A, B, C, M and X, each ten times stronger than the last. An X1 flare corresponds to 10⁻⁴ W/m². The largest flare of the space age, on 4 November 2003, saturated the detectors and has been estimated at about X40.

### Coronal Mass Ejections

A **coronal mass ejection** expels 10¹¹–10¹³ kg of magnetised plasma from the corona at speeds from about 300 to more than 3 000 km/s (@fig:cme). CMEs are observed with **coronagraphs**, which block the bright solar disc to reveal the faint corona. When a fast CME is directed towards the Earth, it typically arrives in one to three days; the fastest have crossed the 150 million km in under 18 hours. The geomagnetic impact of a CME depends above all on the direction of its internal magnetic field. A strong **southward** field component ($B_z$ negative) connects efficiently with the Earth's northward field at the dayside magnetopause. This opens the magnetosphere to the solar wind's energy (Chapter 23).

![A coronal mass ejection erupting from the Sun's limb on 17–18 June 2015, imaged by SDO in extreme ultraviolet light.](figures/photos/ch22_cme.jpg){#fig:cme width=58%}

### Solar Energetic Particles

Flares and the shocks driven by fast CMEs accelerate protons, electrons and heavier ions to energies of millions to billions of electronvolts. These **solar energetic particles** travel along the interplanetary magnetic field and can reach the Earth within tens of minutes. They raise the flux of energetic protons near the Earth by many orders of magnitude for hours to days. The most energetic events produce **ground-level enhancements** detected by neutron monitors at the Earth's surface. SEPs are the main acute radiation hazard for astronauts beyond low Earth orbit and a major cause of single-event effects in spacecraft electronics.

### The Solar Wind

Between eruptions, the corona continuously emits the **solar wind**: about 5 protons and electrons per cubic centimetre at 1 AU. It flows at 300–500 km/s ("slow wind") or 600–800 km/s ("fast wind" from **coronal holes**), carrying the interplanetary magnetic field of about 5 nT. Where fast wind catches up with slow wind, **corotating interaction regions** form; because coronal holes can persist for months, they produce geomagnetic disturbances that recur every 27 days as the Sun rotates.

## Space Weather at the Earth

### Geomagnetic Storms and Indices

When a CME or fast stream with a southward magnetic field reaches the Earth, energy pours into the magnetosphere, driving a **geomagnetic storm**. The ring current around the Earth intensifies and the auroral ovals expand towards the equator. Strong currents flow in the polar ionosphere, and the thermosphere heats and expands. Storms are measured by **geomagnetic indices** derived from networks of ground magnetometers:

- **Kp** (0 to 9, quasi-logarithmic) measures global disturbance in three-hour intervals; **Ap** is its daily linear equivalent.
- **Dst** (in nanotesla) measures the depression of the horizontal magnetic field at low latitudes due to the ring current; major storms reach −200 to −600 nT.
- **AE** measures the intensity of the auroral electrojets.

NOAA's **space weather scales** (@tbl:noaascales) translate these measurements into five levels of severity for geomagnetic storms (G), solar radiation storms (S) and radio blackouts (R).

| Level | Geomagnetic storm (G) | Solar radiation storm (S) | Radio blackout (R) | Approximate frequency per 11-year cycle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Minor | Kp 5 | ≥ 10 pfu (≥ 10 MeV protons) | M1 flare | G1: ≈ 1 700; S1: ≈ 50; R1: ≈ 2 000 |
| 2 Moderate | Kp 6 | ≥ 10² pfu | M5 | G2: ≈ 600; S2: ≈ 25; R2: ≈ 350 |
| 3 Strong | Kp 7 | ≥ 10³ pfu | X1 | G3: ≈ 200; S3: ≈ 10; R3: ≈ 175 |
| 4 Severe | Kp 8 (including 9−) | ≥ 10⁴ pfu | X10 | G4: ≈ 100; S4: ≈ 3; R4: ≈ 8 |
| 5 Extreme | Kp 9 | ≥ 10⁵ pfu | X20 | G5: ≈ 4; S5: < 1; R5: < 1 |

: NOAA space weather scales (pfu = particle flux units, particles cm⁻² s⁻¹ sr⁻¹). {#tbl:noaascales}

The long record of geomagnetic activity (@fig:stormsyears) shows that major storms are concentrated around solar maximum and in the declining phase of each cycle, but that severe storms can occur at almost any time.

### The May 2024 "Gannon" Storm

From 8 May 2024, a cluster of sunspots (region 13664) produced a series of X-class flares and at least six Earth-directed CMEs. They arrived on 10 May and combined to produce the first **G5 (extreme)** geomagnetic storm since 2003 (@fig:may2024), with Kp reaching 9 and Dst falling to about −410 nT. Aurorae were seen as far south as Florida, northern India and Namibia, and photographed by millions of people with smartphones. The storm, named after the solar physicist Jennifer Gannon, caused GNSS-guided tractors to lose accuracy during the planting season in North America. It also degraded HF communications and increased drag on thousands of satellites, causing mass manoeuvres and temporary loss of tracking. In power grids it induced currents, which operators managed without major failures. Further severe storms followed in October 2024, November 2025 and January 2026.

![Three-hourly Kp index during the May 2024 geomagnetic storm (8–14 May).](figures/out/ch22_may2024.svg){#fig:may2024 width=88%}

### Great Storms of the Past

| Date | Event | Notable effects |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 Sept 1859 | Carrington event | First observed white-light flare; telegraph systems failed and caught fire; aurora seen near the equator; Dst estimated −850 to −1 050 nT |
| 13–15 May 1921 | "New York Railroad" storm | Fires in telegraph and railway signalling stations; possibly comparable to Carrington |
| 4 Aug 1972 | Solar particle event and storm | Between Apollo 16 and 17 — a lethal dose risk for astronauts on the Moon; detonated sea mines off Vietnam |
| 13 Mar 1989 | Quebec storm | Hydro-Québec grid collapsed in 92 s, leaving 6 million people without power for 9 hours; tracking of thousands of satellites lost |
| 29–31 Oct 2003 | Halloween storms | X17 and X10 flares (and an estimated X40 on 4 Nov); satellite anomalies and a lost Japanese satellite (ADEOS-II); power outage in Malmö, Sweden; aircraft rerouted; WAAS vertical guidance unavailable for ≈ 30 hours |
| 23 Jul 2012 | Near miss | Extreme CME observed by STEREO-A missed the Earth by about nine days of solar rotation |
| 3–4 Feb 2022 | Minor storm, major loss | Increased drag prevented 38 of 49 newly launched Starlink satellites from raising their orbits |
| 10–11 May 2024 | Gannon storm | First G5 since 2003; worldwide aurorae; GNSS and satellite drag effects |

: Notable space weather events. {#tbl:greatstorms}

Even larger events have occurred. Tree rings and ice cores record sudden increases in radiocarbon and beryllium-10 in 774–775 and 993–994 CE (**Miyake events**). These are attributed to solar particle storms perhaps ten times stronger than any observed in the space age.

## Effects on Technology and People

### Satellites

Space weather affects spacecraft in several ways (Chapter 2):

- **Surface charging**: during storms, hot (keV) electrons injected into the inner magnetosphere charge spacecraft surfaces to thousands of volts, especially in the night-side GEO region. Discharges ("arcs") can damage solar arrays and electronics or cause phantom commands.
- **Internal (deep dielectric) charging**: high-energy (MeV) electrons of the outer radiation belt, which can increase dramatically for days after a storm, penetrate shielding and accumulate in cable insulation and circuit boards until they discharge. Internal charging is blamed for the failures of Canada's Anik E1 and E2 communications satellites in January 1994. It is also blamed for the failure of Galaxy 15 in 2010, which drifted out of control for eight months as a "zombie satellite" still relaying signals.
- **Single-event effects**: solar energetic particles and galactic cosmic rays cause bit flips, latch-ups and burnouts in electronics; star trackers become blinded by particle hits during SEP events.
- **Total ionising dose and displacement damage**: SEP events can deliver in days the dose expected over years, degrading solar cells and sensors.
- **Atmospheric drag**: heating of the thermosphere by storms increases density at satellite altitudes several-fold (@fig:drag). In February 2022, a minor storm prevented 38 of 49 Starlink satellites, released at a low parking altitude, from raising their orbits; they re-entered within days. During major storms, thousands of objects change their orbits unpredictably, degrading the space catalogue and conjunction assessment (Chapter 21).

![Illustrative density of the thermosphere at 400 km as a function of solar EUV activity (F10.7) and geomagnetic activity (Ap). Both solar maximum and storms greatly increase satellite drag.](figures/out/ch22_drag.svg){#fig:drag width=86%}

### GNSS, Radio and Aviation

Disturbances of the ionosphere affect radio signals passing through it (Chapter 23):

- **GNSS errors**: increases and steep gradients of total electron content during storms produce positioning errors of tens of metres for single-frequency users. They also degrade augmentation systems. During the Halloween 2003 storms, WAAS could not provide vertical guidance for about 30 hours.
- **Scintillation**: small-scale irregularities in the ionosphere cause rapid fluctuations of signal amplitude and phase, which can make receivers lose lock. This is common after sunset near the geomagnetic equator and in the auroral and polar regions (@fig:scint).
- **HF radio blackouts**: flare X-rays increase ionisation in the D region, absorbing HF signals on the dayside for minutes to hours. Energetic protons cause **polar cap absorption** lasting days, forcing airlines to reroute polar flights that rely on HF communications.
- **Radiation exposure**: during SEP events, aircrew and passengers on high-latitude, high-altitude flights receive increased radiation doses; aviation authorities issue advisories, and some flights are lowered or rerouted.

![Regions where ionospheric scintillation most often affects GNSS and satellite communications (schematic).](figures/out/ch22_scint.svg){#fig:scint width=96%}

### Power Grids and Other Ground Systems

Rapid changes in the Earth's magnetic field during storms (large $dB/dt$) induce **geoelectric fields** in the conducting ground — typically 1–10 V/km in severe storms, more over resistive rock (@fig:gic). These fields drive **geomagnetically induced currents (GICs)** through long conductors grounded at both ends: high-voltage power lines, pipelines, railways and submarine cables. In power grids, GICs of tens to hundreds of amperes flow through transformer windings into the ground, saturating transformer cores, generating harmonics, causing overheating and tripping protective relays. The March 1989 storm collapsed the Hydro-Québec grid in about 90 seconds and damaged a large transformer at the Salem nuclear plant in New Jersey.

![Geomagnetically induced currents. Ionospheric currents cause rapid magnetic variations that induce an electric field in the ground and drive quasi-DC currents through grounded transformers.](figures/out/ch22_gic.svg){#fig:gic width=94%}

Grid operators now receive storm warnings and run models of GIC flows. They can reduce loading on vulnerable transformers or reconfigure the network. Regulators in North America and Europe require utilities to assess their vulnerability to benchmark extreme storms.

### Astronauts

Outside the protection of the Earth's magnetosphere, astronauts are exposed to SEP events that could deliver dangerous doses in hours. Had the August 1972 event occurred during an Apollo moonwalk, the astronauts could have suffered acute radiation sickness. Crewed spacecraft therefore provide **storm shelters** — areas surrounded by water, food, equipment and propellant — and missions rely on real-time warnings (Chapter 30). Artemis II's Orion spacecraft, flown around the Moon in April 2026, carried radiation sensors and a shelter procedure.

The **L1 Lagrange point**, 1.5 million km sunward of the Earth, is the key location: a spacecraft there measures the solar wind, including the critical direction of its magnetic field, before it reaches the Earth. Because the solar wind takes about 30–60 minutes to travel from L1 to the Earth — and an extreme CME as little as 15 minutes — this provides only short-term warning (@fig:warningtime). Ground-based magnetometers, ionosondes, GNSS networks, neutron monitors and radio telescopes complete the observing system.

![Travel time from L1 to the Earth (minutes) and from the Sun to the Earth (hours) as a function of solar-wind or CME speed.](figures/out/ch22_warningtime.svg){#fig:warningtime width=84%}

### Forecasting

Operational space-weather centres include NOAA's **Space Weather Prediction Center** in Boulder, the UK Met Office Space Weather Operations Centre and ESA's Space Weather Service Network. Others are the regional warning centres of the International Space Environment Service. These centres issue forecasts, watches, warnings and alerts. Their tools include:

- **CME arrival forecasts**: coronagraph images are used to fit a CME's speed and direction, and a heliospheric model such as **WSA–Enlil** propagates it to the Earth. Arrival-time errors are typically ±10 hours.
- **Geomagnetic forecasts**: once a CME reaches L1, measured solar-wind conditions drive magnetospheric models (such as NOAA's Geospace model) that predict Kp, regional magnetic disturbances and GIC risk tens of minutes ahead.
- **Flare and SEP forecasts**: based mainly on the magnetic complexity of active regions; these remain probabilistic and of limited skill.
- **Ionosphere and thermosphere models** for GNSS users and satellite operators.

The greatest limitation is that the magnetic orientation of a CME, which determines its geomagnetic impact, cannot yet be measured until it reaches L1. Missions viewing the Sun from the side (L5), new methods of measuring coronal magnetic fields and machine-learning models are among the approaches to improve forecasts.

### Space Weather Beyond the Earth

Space weather affects all planets. During the May 2024 storms, NASA's MAVEN spacecraft observed global aurorae on Mars, and its Curiosity rover measured a radiation spike on the surface (@fig:marsaurora). Mars lacks a global magnetic field, so it is fully exposed to SEPs — a key concern for future crews.

![Ultraviolet aurorae across the night side of Mars (purple) observed by NASA's MAVEN orbiter during the solar storm of May 2024.](figures/photos/ch22_maven.jpg){#fig:marsaurora width=48%}

## Designing for Space Weather

Engineers design spacecraft and infrastructure for specified space-weather environments:

- **Environment specifications** such as ECSS-E-ST-10-04 and NASA and ISO standards define radiation-belt fluxes, SEP fluences, charging environments and thermospheric density ranges, often including a "worst case" or 1-in-100-year event.
- **Spacecraft design**: radiation-tolerant components and error-correcting memories; shielding of sensitive parts; grounding and conductive coatings to prevent charging; derating of solar arrays for degradation; propellant margins for higher drag; autonomous safe modes.
- **Operations**: delaying launches, EVAs or critical manoeuvres during storms; placing satellites in safe configurations; increasing tracking and conjunction screening.
- **Ground infrastructure**: GIC-blocking devices and transformer designs for power grids; GNSS receivers with multi-frequency and integrity monitoring; alternative timing sources.

::: example
**Example: estimating a geomagnetically induced current**

During a severe storm, a ground magnetometer in northern Europe records magnetic variations of about 1 000 nT with a period of about 10 minutes (ω = 2π/600 s ≈ 0.0105 s⁻¹). For a plane wave over uniform ground of conductivity σ = 10⁻³ S/m, the induced electric field is

$$E \approx B\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{\mu_0\sigma}} = 10^{-6}\ \text{T}\times\sqrt{\frac{0.0105}{1.26\times10^{-6}\times10^{-3}}} \approx 2.9\times10^{-3}\ \text{V/m} \approx 3\ \text{V/km}.$$

Over more resistive rock, or during faster variations, the field can be several times larger.

Taking E = 5 V/km along a 200 km high-voltage line, the driving voltage is 1 000 V. The loop consists of the line, two transformer windings and two substation earthings. If its total resistance is about 5 Ω, a quasi-DC current of about 200 A flows through each transformer neutral. This is enough to drive a large transformer into half-cycle saturation, causing heating, reactive-power demand and harmonics. Grid operators therefore monitor neutral currents and may reduce transformer loading during severe storms.
:::

::: ai
**AI in space weather**

Machine learning is used to predict flares from magnetograms, estimate CME arrival times, forecast geomagnetic indices from solar-wind data, nowcast GIC and ionospheric scintillation, and model thermospheric density for satellite drag. NASA and NOAA have explored deep-learning models that predict regional geomagnetic disturbances about 30 minutes ahead from L1 data. Chapter 47 discusses these applications.
:::

::: key
**Key takeaways**

- Space weather comprises solar flares (X-rays in 8 minutes), energetic particles (minutes to hours) and CMEs (1–3 days) that disturb the Earth's space environment.
- The Sun's magnetic activity follows an ~11-year sunspot cycle; Cycle 25 peaked at a smoothed sunspot number of about 161 in October 2024, well above forecast.
- Geomagnetic storms are measured by Kp, Ap and Dst and classified by NOAA's G scale; the May 2024 Gannon storm was the first G5 since 2003.
- Effects include satellite charging, radiation damage and drag (38 Starlink satellites lost in 2022), GNSS errors and scintillation, HF blackouts, aviation and astronaut radiation, and geomagnetically induced currents in power grids (Quebec, 1989).
- L1 monitors give 15–60 minutes of warning of a storm's severity; coronagraphs give 1–3 days of warning of CME arrival but not of its magnetic orientation.
- New observatories launched in 2024–2025 (Proba-3, PUNCH, SWFO-L1, IMAP) and a planned L5 mission (Vigil) aim to improve forecasts.
:::
