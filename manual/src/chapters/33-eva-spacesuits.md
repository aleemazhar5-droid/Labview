**Extravehicular activity (EVA)** — a spacewalk — is any time a crew member leaves the protection of a pressurised vehicle and works in a spacesuit in vacuum or on another world's surface. A spacesuit used this way is the smallest crewed spacecraft there is: wrapped closely around one person, it must provide pressure, oxygen, carbon dioxide removal, cooling, power, communications, protection from radiation, temperature extremes and micrometeoroids, and enough mobility for hands to do precise work for a full working day.

People go outside because some jobs still need hands and eyes on the spot. Spacewalkers built the International Space Station (ISS), upgraded the Hubble Space Telescope five times, saved Skylab and collected 382 kg of rock on the Moon. By October 2026 there had been 284 spacewalks at the ISS alone, and the longest single EVA had reached 9 h 06 min. This chapter covers the history, the suit and its backpack, decompression, operations and the suits being built for the Moon and Mars. Life support in general is covered in Chapter 30, crewed vehicles in Chapter 31, stations and airlocks in Chapter 32, and robotic servicing in Chapter 40.

## Sixty Years of Spacewalks

The history of EVA is a history of learning how hard it is. @fig:evasuits shows the suits that have been used outside and their years of service, and @tbl:suitcomparison compares the main ones.

### First Steps Outside: Voskhod and Gemini

On 18 March 1965 Alexei Leonov left Voskhod 2 through an inflatable airlock and floated for about 12 minutes at the end of a tether. His Berkut suit, pressurised to 0.4 atmospheres, ballooned so much in vacuum that his feet and hands pulled away from the boots and gloves, and he could not fit back into the airlock. Without telling the ground, he bled the suit down to about 0.27 atmospheres — accepting a risk of decompression sickness — squeezed in head first, and only then could turn round to close the hatch. The first lesson of EVA: a pressurised suit is a balloon whose shape must be controlled.

Ed White followed on Gemini 4 on 3 June 1965 (@fig:edwhite), using a hand-held gas gun to manoeuvre for some 20 minutes. Most of the Gemini spacewalks that followed proved far harder than expected. On Gemini 9A in June 1966 Eugene Cernan struggled for two hours to don a manoeuvring backpack stowed on the spacecraft's adapter. With nothing to brace against, every push moved his body rather than the task; his heart rate reportedly reached about 180 beats per minute and his visor fogged until he could barely see. Richard Gordon on Gemini 11 was similarly exhausted.

![Ed White during the first American spacewalk, Gemini 4, 3 June 1965. The gold-coated visor protects his eyes from sunlight; the umbilical supplies oxygen and a tether; he holds the hand-held manoeuvring unit.](figures/photos/ch33_white.jpg){#fig:edwhite width=52%}

The solution came on Gemini 12 in November 1966. Buzz Aldrin trained underwater beforehand, and his spacecraft carried handrails, foot restraints ("golden slippers"), waist tethers and rest periods written into the plan. He worked for about 5.5 hours over three EVAs without overexertion. Restraints, neutral-buoyancy training and paced timelines are still the foundation of EVA practice.

### Apollo and Skylab

The Apollo **A7L** suit, built by ILC Dover, worked at 25.8 kPa (3.75 psi) of pure oxygen. For the Moon it was combined with a **portable life support system (PLSS)** backpack that supplied oxygen, scrubbed carbon dioxide with lithium hydroxide and cooled the astronaut by sublimating water to vacuum — the architecture of every backpack since. On the Moon, twelve astronauts spent about 80 hours outside in 14 EVAs between July 1969 and December 1972 (see the section on other worlds below).

In May 1973 the Skylab space station reached orbit with its micrometeoroid shield torn away and one solar wing missing and the other jammed. On 7 June Pete Conrad and Joe Kerwin, in Apollo-type suits fed by umbilicals, cut the restraining strap and freed the remaining wing in a 3 h 25 min EVA — the first demonstration that spacewalkers could rescue a crippled spacecraft.

### The Shuttle, the MMU and Hubble

The Space Shuttle brought the **Extravehicular Mobility Unit (EMU)**, built by Hamilton Standard and ILC Dover, first used by Story Musgrave and Don Peterson on STS-6 in April 1983. Unlike the custom-tailored Apollo suits, the EMU is assembled from standard-sized parts, so a fleet of parts can fit many astronauts. Its PLSS is mounted on the hard upper torso, and it works at 29.6 kPa (4.3 psi).

On 7 February 1984 Bruce McCandless flew the **Manned Manoeuvring Unit (MMU)**, a nitrogen-gas jet backpack with 24 thrusters, up to about 98 m from Challenger with no tether at all (@fig:mmuflight). MMUs were used on two more flights in 1984, to help repair the Solar Maximum Mission satellite and to capture two stranded communications satellites, and were then retired: the Shuttle's arm and tethered crews could do the same work with less risk.

The **Hubble Space Telescope** turned EVA into a precision trade. Hubble was designed to be serviced: its instruments slide out on rails, its electronics boxes have captive bolts, and handrails and foot-restraint sockets cover its exterior. Five servicing missions between 1993 and 2009 made 23 spacewalks totalling 166 hours, replacing instruments, gyroscopes, batteries and solar arrays and fixing the telescope's flawed optics (@fig:hubbleeva). On the final mission astronauts repaired two instruments never designed to be opened, removing more than a hundred tiny screws.

### Building and Keeping the Space Station

The ISS was assembled in orbit between 1998 and 2011, and spacewalks connected every module, truss segment and cable that robots could not. @fig:issevas shows the result: from December 1998 to September 2026, there were 284 spacewalks totalling about 1 790 hours; NASA's itemised list shows a peak of 23 in 2007, when Shuttle assembly flights and station crews worked outside almost monthly. Since the Shuttle's retirement, spacewalks have mostly been maintenance — replacing pumps, batteries and failed electronics, installing new roll-out solar arrays — at a rate of about nine a year. The longest ISS EVA, by Jim Voss and Susan Helms in March 2001, lasted 8 h 56 min.

![Spacewalks at the International Space Station per year and hours spent outside, December 1998 to September 2026. The 2024 dip followed water leaks and other faults in the ageing US suits; US spacewalks resumed in 2025–26.](figures/out/ch33_issevas.svg){#fig:issevas width=96%}

In 2024 only three spacewalks took place: one US EVA in June ended after 31 minutes when water leaked from a service-and-cooling umbilical, and NASA paused others while suit faults were investigated. Seven spacewalks followed in 2026 up to September, including a seven-hour repair of a wrist joint of the station's Canadarm2 robotic arm.

### Russia, China and the First Commercial Spacewalk

The Soviet **Orlan** suits, first used outside Salyut 6 in December 1977, are semi-rigid: a single-piece aluminium torso with a rear hatch formed by the hinged backpack, so a cosmonaut climbs in from behind in a few minutes without help. The current **Orlan-MKS**, in use on the ISS since 2017, works at 40 kPa, has about 7 hours of autonomy, an onboard computer and automatic thermal control, and is rated for 15–20 spacewalks over five to six years (@fig:orlaneva). Russia delivered new Orlan-MKS suits to the ISS in 2025 and 2026.

China's **Feitian** suit was derived from the Orlan. Zhai Zhigang wore the first version for China's first spacewalk, about 20 minutes long, from Shenzhou-7 on 27 September 2008. The second generation, in use at the Tiangong station since July 2021, supports up to 8 hours outside; Chinese sources report that its design life has been extended from 15 to 20 spacewalks. On 17 December 2024 Cai Xuzhe and Song Lingdong of Shenzhou-19 stayed outside for 9 h 06 min, the longest spacewalk ever, and by mid-2026 Chinese crews had made more than 25 spacewalks, many to fit debris shields to the station.

![Cosmonaut Alexey Zubritsky in an Orlan suit during a Russian spacewalk on 28 October 2025. The blue stripes distinguish the two cosmonauts; the bulky backpack is also the suit's entry hatch.](figures/photos/ch33_orlan.jpg){#fig:orlaneva width=56%}

The first commercial spacewalk took place on 12 September 2024 on the privately funded Polaris Dawn mission. Crew Dragon has no airlock, so after about two days of prebreathe with the cabin at reduced pressure, all four crew members suited up and the whole cabin was vented to vacuum. Jared Isaacman and SpaceX engineer Sarah Gillis in turn leaned out of the hatch to test the mobility of the new SpaceX EVA suit, which worked at about 34 kPa (5 psi) and drew oxygen, power and data through 3.7 m umbilicals rather than a backpack. From depressurisation to repressurisation the EVA lasted 1 h 46 min. No second commercial spacewalk had flown by October 2026.

## Anatomy of a Spacesuit

A spacesuit has two main parts: the **pressure garment**, which holds the atmosphere around the body, and the life support system, which keeps that atmosphere breathable and cool. @fig:evasuit shows the main elements of the US EMU and the layers of its wall.

![Main elements of the US Extravehicular Mobility Unit and, right, the layers of the suit wall from the skin outwards. The bladder holds the gas, the restraint carries the pressure load, and the thermal and micrometeoroid garment protects both.](figures/out/ch33_suit.svg){#fig:evasuit width=98%}

### Why Suits Work at Low Pressure

The suit pressure is the most important number in its design. It must be high enough to supply oxygen: with pure oxygen, the oxygen pressure in the lungs is the suit pressure minus about 6.3 kPa of water vapour and 5.3 kPa of carbon dioxide. Matching the 13.3 kPa of a person breathing sea-level air needs about 25 kPa — almost exactly the Apollo suit pressure (@fig:suitpressure a). Below about 6.3 kPa (the **Armstrong limit**) body fluids boil at body temperature, so even an emergency suit pressure must stay well above it.

Every kilopascal above the minimum makes the suit stiffer. An inflated fabric tube resists bending because bending it reduces its volume, and the gas must be compressed to make room. A simple model gives the moment needed to fold an unjointed tube of radius $r$ at pressure $p$:

$$ M \approx \pi\, p\, r^3 $$

For an elbow-sized tube, 5.5 cm in radius, this is about 15 N·m at 29.6 kPa and 30 N·m at 56.5 kPa; for a knee it is three times larger (@fig:suitpressure b). An astronaut flexing an arm hundreds of times a day against such moments tires within an hour. Low pressure is therefore the first defence of mobility, and clever joints are the second. The price of low pressure is decompression sickness, discussed below.

![Why suits are pressurised to 26–57 kPa. (a) Oxygen pressure in the lungs for a pure-oxygen suit: about 25 kPa matches breathing sea-level air. (b) Moment needed to fold a plain inflated tube of different radii, which grows in proportion to pressure.](figures/out/ch33_pressure.svg){#fig:suitpressure width=98%}

### Layers

The wall of a suit has three functional groups (@fig:evasuit). Next to the skin, a **liquid cooling and ventilation garment (LCVG)** — a stretch-mesh undergarment threaded with about 90 m of thin plastic tubing in the EMU — carries cool water over the body and collects ventilation gas from the limbs. Over it the **pressure garment** has two layers: a gas-tight **bladder** of urethane-coated nylon, and a **restraint layer** of strong polyester fabric that carries the pressure load and fixes the suit's shape, so the bladder never stretches. Outside is the **thermal micrometeoroid garment (TMG)**: a neoprene-coated nylon liner, several layers of aluminised film acting as multilayer insulation (Chapter 5), and an outer shell of Ortho-Fabric, a weave of Gore-Tex, Kevlar and Nomex that resists abrasion, fire and small particles. The outer surface can exceed +120 °C in sunlight and fall below −150 °C in shadow.

### Joints and Mobility

The aim of joint design is a **constant-volume joint**: one whose internal volume, and therefore the work done against the gas, stays nearly the same as it bends. Three techniques are used. **Convolutes** are bellows-like folds that fill on the inside of the bend as the outside opens. **Flat-pattern joints** use sewn gores and restraint cables or webbing along the axis of the joint, so the fabric on one side shortens as the other lengthens. **Rotary bearings** — sealed rings at the shoulder, upper arm, wrist, waist and hip — let a limb rotate with almost no change in volume. Hard parts carry loads: the EMU's **hard upper torso** is a fibreglass shell to which the arms, helmet, lower torso and backpack attach, and the Orlan, Feitian and AxEMU extend this to a rear-entry hard torso.

Fit matters as much as design: in a suit that is too large the joints of suit and body no longer coincide; one too small presses on shoulders and knees. In a NASA review, 14 of 22 astronauts reported shoulder injuries related to EVA training or flight, many from working in the EMU's hard torso with arms overhead in the pool.

### Gloves and Hands

Gloves are the hardest part of a suit to design: the hand has many small joints and weak muscles, and a glove must be pressurised, insulated and tough enough for sharp edges. EMU gloves are custom-sized from casts or scans of each astronaut's hands. They have a bladder and restraint with finger joints and a wrist bearing, heated fingertips for cold work, and rubberised palms. Even so, grasping with a pressurised glove takes much more effort than with a bare hand and fatigues the forearms within hours. In one NASA study, 770 suited training events produced 352 reported symptoms, 47 % of them in the hands; the most common was **fingernail delamination**, the loss of nails, attributed to moisture in the gloves and loads on the fingertips. Gloves are also vulnerable to damage: after a cut glove ended a spacewalk early in 2007, crews began photographing their gloves during spacewalks, and gloves are inspected after every EVA (@fig:airlock).

![Astronauts Mike Foreman (left) and Rick Linnehan in the ISS Quest airlock after a spacewalk in March 2008, still wearing their EMUs. Gloves are photographed and inspected for cuts after each spacewalk.](figures/photos/ch33_gloves.jpg){#fig:airlock width=60%}

### Helmet and Visors

The EMU helmet is a clear polycarbonate bubble. Over it fits the **extravehicular visor assembly**, with a clear protective visor and a gold-coated sun visor that reflects visible light and infrared, plus adjustable eyeshades. Lights and video cameras are mounted on the sides. Inside are a microphone and earphones in a fabric "Snoopy cap", a drink bag of about 0.95 L and a foam block against which the astronaut can press the nose to clear the ears. The SpaceX EVA suit added a head-up display and a copper and indium tin oxide visor coating.

## The Portable Life Support System

The **portable life support system (PLSS)** turns a pressure garment into a spacecraft. @fig:plssflow shows its two loops. In the **ventilation loop**, a fan blows oxygen into the helmet at the face; the gas sweeps over the head and body, collects carbon dioxide, moisture and heat, and is drawn back from the wrists and ankles through a canister that removes carbon dioxide and odours, then through the sublimator, which cools it and condenses moisture, and a water separator, before returning to the helmet. In the **cooling-water loop**, a pump circulates water through the LCVG tubes and the sublimator. The EMU's fan, pump and separator are a single rotating machine.

![Simplified flow diagram of the EMU portable life support system. Oxygen is recirculated through a carbon dioxide canister and a sublimator; cooling water circulates through the garment; feedwater is lost to space as vapour.](figures/out/ch33_plss.svg){#fig:plssflow width=98%}

### Oxygen and Pressure

The suit is a closed loop, so oxygen is needed only to replace what the astronaut consumes and what leaks out. An astronaut working at 250 W consumes about 63 g of oxygen per hour. The EMU's primary tanks hold about 0.54 kg at 6.2 MPa; a regulator keeps the suit at 29.6 kPa. A **secondary oxygen pack** of about 1.2 kg at 41 MPa can maintain pressure for about 30 minutes in **purge mode**, flushing oxygen straight through the suit to remove carbon dioxide and heat if the fan fails. This is the "get-back" time: EVA plans keep the astronaut within about 30 minutes of the airlock.

### Removing Carbon Dioxide

Apollo backpacks and early Shuttle EMUs absorbed carbon dioxide in disposable **lithium hydroxide (LiOH)** cartridges (the chemistry is described in Chapter 30). The ISS EMU uses regenerable **metal oxide (METOX)** canisters based on silver oxide, which absorb about 0.54 kg of carbon dioxide and are then regenerated in an oven on the station at about 200 °C. The next generation, used in NASA's xEMU prototype and the AxEMU, uses a **rapid cycle amine (RCA)** swing bed: two small beds of amine sorbent alternate every few minutes, one absorbing carbon dioxide and water while the other is exposed to vacuum to release them. Because the beds regenerate continuously, CO₂ capacity no longer limits EVA duration (@tbl:plsstech).

::: example
**Example: carbon dioxide removal for a 7-hour EVA**

An astronaut works for 7 hours at an average metabolic rate of 250 W, with a respiratory quotient (CO₂ produced ÷ O₂ consumed) of 0.85.

1. Oxygen: at RQ 0.85 each litre of oxygen releases about 20.4 kJ (Weir equation), so 250 W needs 250 × 3 600 / 20 400 = **44.1 L/h** of O₂ (at 0 °C, 1 atm), or 63 g/h.
2. Carbon dioxide: 0.85 × 44.1 = 37.5 L/h = **73.6 g/h**; over 7 hours **515 g**, or 589 g if a 1-hour reserve is added.
3. Lithium hydroxide absorbs at most 0.92 kg of CO₂ per kg (Chapter 30), so 589 g needs at least **0.64 kg**; real beds, never fully used, need about twice that. The Apollo 15–17 backpacks carried 1.42 kg.
4. A METOX canister with 0.54 kg capacity lasts 0.54/0.0736 = **7.3 h** at 250 W, but only 6.3 h at 293 W — one reason heavy work shortens EVAs (@fig:evametab b).
5. Ventilation: the EMU fan moves about 0.17 m³/min (6 cubic feet per minute) of gas at 29.6 kPa, or 0.034 mol/s. Carbon dioxide is added at 4.6 × 10⁻⁴ mol/s, so the returning gas carries about 1.4 % more CO₂ than the gas entering the helmet — a rise of **0.40 kPa (3 mmHg)**. Fresh gas is therefore aimed at the face, where exhaled CO₂ is concentrated.
:::

### Rejecting Heat

The astronaut's metabolic heat, typically 200–300 W and sometimes much more, must be removed without air to carry it away. A radiator would be far too large (see the example below), so the PLSS throws away water. In a **sublimator**, feedwater flows to one side of a porous metal plate whose other side faces vacuum. Water in the pores freezes, and the ice sublimates — turns directly to vapour — absorbing 2.83 MJ per kilogram. The process regulates itself: more heat sublimates ice faster. NASA's newer **spacesuit water membrane evaporator (SWME)**, used in the xEMU design, evaporates water through hydrophobic hollow fibres and is less sensitive to contamination, which has blocked sublimator plates in the past.

::: example
**Example: water for a sublimator**

The PLSS must reject 300 W for a 7-hour EVA using a sublimator (latent heat 2.83 MJ/kg).

1. Water rate: 300 / 2.83 × 10⁶ = 1.06 × 10⁻⁴ kg/s = **0.38 kg/h**.
2. For 7 hours: **2.7 kg**. The EMU carries about 3.8 kg of feedwater, enough for 10 hours at 300 W but only 6.4 hours at the 470 W for which the suit is sized for short periods.
3. Apollo's extended backpacks carried 5.2 kg of water for 8-hour lunar EVAs, an average capacity of 5.2 × 2.83 × 10⁶ / 28 800 ≈ **510 W** — more than the EMU, for the heavier work of lunar surface EVAs.
4. A suit radiator would need 300 / (0.9 × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 280⁴) ≈ **0.96 m²** of surface viewing cold space — about the area of the whole suit, half of which faces the Sun or the ground at any time.
:::

At such rates the CO₂ canister and the oxygen run out before the water, and all three give roughly the 7 hours plus reserve for which the EMU is rated. A 2024 NASA review concluded that EVAs of 6.5 hours are reasonable while average metabolic rates stay below about 290 W (1 000 Btu/h).

### Power, Communications and Displays

A rechargeable battery powers the fan, pump, radios, sensors, lights and cameras. The EMU's **display and control module** on the chest carries the oxygen and cooling controls and a small text display; astronauts read its labels through a wrist mirror, and checklists are worn on the cuff. A caution-and-warning system monitors pressure, oxygen, carbon dioxide, battery and water, and suit data are radioed to the ground with voice and video. Newer suits add colour and head-up displays and record health data such as heart rate.

### SAFER: the Self-Rescue Jet Pack

A broken tether could leave an astronaut drifting away from the station with no one able to follow. The **Simplified Aid For EVA Rescue (SAFER)** is a small nitrogen-jet backpack, about 38 kg, that latches under the EMU's PLSS. It has 24 small thrusters, a hand controller that folds out from its lower edge and an automatic attitude hold that stops tumbling at the push of a button. Its 1.4 kg of nitrogen gives a velocity change of only about 3 m/s — enough to stop, turn and fly back a short distance, but not to explore. SAFER was first tested on STS-64 in September 1994 (@fig:safertest), is worn on every US spacewalk at the ISS, and has never had to be used in an emergency.

## Decompression Sickness and Prebreathe

### Nitrogen in the Tissues

The ISS cabin holds sea-level air, so the body's tissues are saturated with nitrogen at about 80 kPa. If an astronaut moves straight to a suit at 29.6 kPa, the nitrogen becomes supersaturated and can form bubbles — **decompression sickness (DCS)**, introduced in Chapter 30. Mild DCS is pain in the joints; serious ("Type II") DCS affects the brain, spinal cord or lungs. NASA uses the **tissue ratio** $R$ — tissue nitrogen pressure divided by suit pressure — as an index of risk:

$$ R = \frac{P_{N_2,\,\text{tissue}}}{P_\text{suit}}, \qquad P_{N_2,\,\text{tissue}}(t) = P_{N_2,\,\text{amb}} + \bigl(P_{N_2,0} - P_{N_2,\,\text{amb}}\bigr)\, 2^{-t/t_{1/2}} $$

The tissue relaxes towards the nitrogen pressure it breathes with a half-time $t_{1/2}$ of 360 minutes, the value that best matched NASA and US Air Force chamber tests. Breathing pure oxygen drops the inspired nitrogen to almost zero, so nitrogen leaves the tissues. For the EMU at 29.6 kPa, protocols with $R \le 1.65$ have historically been accepted. NASA's standard requires that any protocol be shown in ground trials to give less than a 4 % probability of DCS with 95 % confidence.

Without prebreathe, R is 2.70 for the EMU, 2.32 for a 34.5 kPa suit and 2.00 for the 40 kPa Orlan. @fig:prebreathe a shows the oxygen prebreathe needed to reach R = 1.65: 4.3 hours from sea-level air for the EMU, 3.0 hours at 34.5 kPa and 1.7 hours for the Orlan; above 48.5 kPa none is needed. Russian practice accepts a shorter prebreathe, and so a higher R: cosmonauts breathe oxygen in the Orlan for only about 30 minutes before going out. A low-pressure, oxygen-rich cabin changes the picture completely. Living at 70.3 kPa with 26.5 % oxygen, the "campout" atmosphere, leaves only 52 kPa of nitrogen in the tissues; NASA's 56.5 kPa, 34 % oxygen **exploration atmosphere** leaves 37 kPa, R = 1.26, and needs no prebreathe at all.

(b) R during two Shuttle-era protocols, compared with living in the exploration atmosphere.](figures/out/ch33_dcs.svg){#fig:prebreathe width=98%}

### Prebreathe Protocols

Four hours in a suit breathing oxygen wastes crew time, so shorter protocols were developed (@tbl:prebreathetbl). The Shuttle's **staged decompression** lowered the whole cabin to 70.3 kPa for at least 12 hours before EVA days; in practice crews spent an average of more than 40 hours at this pressure. The ISS cannot lower its whole cabin, so two other approaches are used. In the **campout**, the EVA crew sleep in the closed Quest airlock at 70.3 kPa for up to 8 h 40 min before the EVA. In **exercise prebreathe**, the astronaut breathes oxygen while pedalling vigorously; exercise increases blood flow and speeds nitrogen removal, cutting the required time to about two hours. The **in-suit light exercise (ISLE)** protocol replaces hard exercise with light movement in the suit after 40 minutes on a mask and a 20-minute partial depressurisation. NASA has never had a Type II DCS case in spaceflight, and no DCS was reported in more than 140 Shuttle EVAs.

| Campout | night in airlock at 70.3 kPa (≤ 8 h 40 min); O₂ in suit | overnight + ≈ 1 h | ISS |

::: example
**Example: R-value for the Shuttle staged protocol**

Start from tissue nitrogen in equilibrium with sea-level air: 0.79 × 101.3 = 80.0 kPa. Use $t_{1/2}$ = 360 min and a suit at 29.6 kPa.

1. No prebreathe: R = 80.0/29.6 = **2.70** — unacceptable.
2. 60 min of pure oxygen at 101.3 kPa: $80.0 \times 2^{-60/360}$ = **71.3 kPa**.
3. 12 h in a cabin at 70.3 kPa with 26.5 % O₂, where inspired nitrogen is 0.735 × 70.3 = 51.7 kPa: $51.7 + (71.3 - 51.7) \times 2^{-720/360}$ = **56.6 kPa**.
4. 75 min of oxygen in the suit: $56.6 \times 2^{-75/360}$ = **49.0 kPa**, so R = 49.0/29.6 = **1.65** — exactly the accepted limit.
5. By comparison, 4 hours of in-suit oxygen from sea-level air gives $80.0 \times 2^{-240/360}$ = 50.4 kPa, R = **1.70** (@fig:prebreathe b); the real 4-hour protocol was validated by chamber tests rather than by R alone.
:::

### The Pressure–Mobility Trade

The choice of suit pressure is a three-way trade between oxygen supply, mobility and decompression (@fig:suitpressure and @fig:prebreathe). A higher-pressure suit needs no prebreathe but is stiffer, heavier and leakier. The answer adopted for NASA's xEMU, and inherited by the AxEMU, is a **variable-pressure suit** that can run at 29.6 kPa for mobility or up to 56.5 kPa to shorten prebreathe or treat suspected DCS. For frequent lunar or Martian surface EVAs, a reduced-pressure, oxygen-enriched habitat combined with a suit at 29.6–34 kPa is the preferred direction, limited mainly by the fire risk of oxygen-rich cabins (Chapter 30).

## Working Outside

### Tools, Tethers and Restraints

The first rule of EVA is never to let go. Each astronaut is clipped to the structure by a retractable **safety tether** of about 26 m on a reel, and moves hand over hand along handrails, attaching a **waist tether** at each worksite. Every tool and part is tethered too; lost objects become orbital debris (Chapter 21). For two-handed work the astronaut locks the boots into an **articulating portable foot restraint**, which fits sockets on the structure or the end of the robotic arm. The station's Canadarm2 (Chapter 32) can carry an astronaut and a large unit, such as a battery or pump, from one worksite to another, as the Shuttle arm did at Hubble (@fig:hubbleeva).

Tools are designed for bulky gloves. The **pistol grip tool**, a battery-powered torque-limited wrench, does most bolt work; bolts are captive, connectors have large tabs, and replaceable units have guide pins and handles placed for a gloved hand (@fig:iss2026eva).

### Training in Water

The best simulation of weightlessness on Earth is neutral buoyancy. NASA's **Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory (NBL)** in Houston, built in the mid-1990s, is a pool 62 m long, 31 m wide and 12 m deep holding 23.5 million litres of water and full-size mockups of ISS modules. Suited astronauts are weighted until they neither float nor sink and rehearse every task with support divers, for several hours per hour of planned EVA (@fig:nbltraining). Water drag and gravity inside the suit make the pool imperfect, so it is complemented by virtual reality, air-bearing floors and, for lunar suits, reduced-gravity rigs. Russia and China have similar hydrolabs.

### Timelines and Workload

A typical ISS spacewalk lasts 6–7 hours, plus about as much again for suit checks, prebreathe, depressurisation and post-EVA servicing. The timeline is planned to the minute, with "get-ahead" tasks and pre-agreed stopping points. Flight controllers estimate metabolic rate from oxygen use and cooling-water temperatures, because consumables and heat limit the day (@fig:evametab). NASA measures **EVA efficiency** as EVA time divided by the total crew time it consumes; for ISS protocols this is only about 0.4–0.5.

### When Things Go Wrong

Serious EVA incidents have been few, but each taught a lesson (@tbl:evaincidents). The most dangerous at the ISS came on 16 July 2013, during Luca Parmitano's EVA 23. About 44 minutes in he reported water at the back of his head; it spread over his eyes, nose and ears, and he found his way back to the airlock largely by feel before the EVA ended at 92 minutes. Between 1 and 1.5 litres of water had entered the helmet from the ventilation loop. The investigation traced it to contaminants blocking holes in the fan/pump/separator, and found that a smaller leak on the previous EVA had been misdiagnosed as a leaking drink bag. NASA added absorbent pads and a snorkel to the helmet and tightened the handling of suit anomalies.

| Year | Event | Lesson |
|--|----------|--------|
| 1965 | Leonov's suit balloons; he lowers pressure to re-enter the airlock | control of suit shape; airlock sizing |
| 1973 | Skylab solar wing freed with improvised tools | EVA as a contingency capability |
| 2007 | Torn ISS solar array repaired from the end of a boom extension | improvised tools; rehearse with ground teams |
| 2013 | EVA 23: about 1.5 L of water in Parmitano's helmet | treat suit anomalies as life-threatening |
| 2024 | ISS EVA ends after 31 min: water leak in suit umbilical | ageing suits limit operations |

## Spacewalking on Other Worlds

### Lessons from Apollo

Gravity on the Moon is one sixth of Earth's, so the 82–96 kg Apollo suit and backpack felt like only 14–16 kg, but the suit's stiff legs and the low traction made normal walking awkward. Astronauts found that a **loping** gait, bounding from foot to foot, or a two-footed hop, was faster and less tiring, and falls were common but usually harmless. Apollo 11–14 crews walked, with backpacks limited to 4 hours; Apollo 15–17 carried the **Lunar Roving Vehicle (LRV)** and 8-hour backpacks. @fig:lunareva shows how surface time and range grew: the Apollo 17 crew spent 22 h 04 min outside over three EVAs and drove 35.9 km, up to 7.6 km from their lander.

![Apollo surface EVAs. (a) Time outside per landing, by EVA. (b) Distances driven with the Lunar Roving Vehicle on Apollo 15–17; the farthest point from the lander was limited by the need to be able to walk back if the rover failed.](figures/out/ch33_lunar.svg){#fig:lunareva width=98%}

The rover was a safety system as well as a vehicle. Traverses were planned so that, if the rover broke down at the farthest point, both astronauts could walk back to the lander within the consumables left in their backpacks, with the backup oxygen supply as a last reserve. This **walkback limit** is the surface equivalent of the 30-minute get-back time in orbit, and it will constrain Artemis traverses too until pressurised rovers are available.

Lunar dust was the biggest surprise. The regolith is made of sharp, glassy fragments never rounded by wind or water, and it clings electrostatically to everything. After three days on the Moon, Apollo 17 suits were grey to the knees, their bearings were harder to turn and their outer fabric was abraded; dust in the cabin irritated the crew's eyes and lungs (@fig:cernandust). Gloves wore fastest, and astronauts' hands and fingers were sore after hours of gripping tools.

### Artemis Suits

NASA decided in 2022 to buy lunar and station suits as a service rather than build them, under its **Exploration Extravehicular Activity Services (xEVAS)** contracts. Collins Aerospace withdrew in 2024, leaving Axiom Space as the sole provider. Axiom's **AxEMU** is derived from NASA's xEMU prototype (@fig:axemuphoto): a rear-entry suit with a hard upper torso, interchangeable arms and legs to fit people from the 1st to the 99th percentile in size, mobility joints at the hips and knees that let the wearer kneel and squat, and an xEMU-style backpack with a rapid-cycle-amine scrubber and a water membrane evaporator. Its goals include EVAs of at least 8 hours and at least two hours in the permanently shadowed craters near the lunar south pole, where temperatures fall below −200 °C.

By early 2026 Axiom had completed its own design review and more than 850 hours of crewed pressurised tests. A year-long qualification campaign was planned to run to mid-2027, with a first suit flying in Earth orbit in 2027 for checkout. In April 2026 NASA's Office of Inspector General warned that the schedule had little or no margin for the first landing, now planned for Artemis IV in 2028, and that development times averaging 8.7 years for past suits would imply a delay to about 2031. In September 2026 NASA and Axiom were reported to be studying a simpler, lighter suit configuration for the first landing. China, meanwhile, unveiled its **Wangyu** lunar suit in 2024 for a crewed landing planned before 2030.

### Suits for Mars

Mars adds problems that the Moon does not (@tbl:moonmarsenv). Its gravity, 0.38 g, makes suit mass more than twice as burdensome as on the Moon, so a Mars suit must be much lighter than the AxEMU. Its thin carbon dioxide atmosphere, about 0.6 kPa, carries heat by convection, so insulation that works in vacuum works less well. The surface pressure is close to the triple point of water, 0.61 kPa, so a sublimator will not work reliably, and evaporative coolers lose efficiency. An amine scrubber that regenerates by exposure to "vacuum" must vent against an atmosphere that is itself 95 % carbon dioxide, so it needs heating or a different sorbent. Martian dust is finer than lunar dust, is lifted by wind and contains perchlorates, which are toxic. Suits must also be maintained and repaired on site for months.

| Factor | ISS orbit | Moon | Mars |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gravity | ≈ 0 | 0.17 g | 0.38 g |
| Ambient pressure | vacuum | vacuum | ≈ 0.6 kPa CO₂ |
| Heat rejection | sublimator | sublimator | evaporator or radiator; sublimator unreliable |
| Dust | none | sharp, electrostatic | fine, windblown, perchlorates |
| Return to safety | ≈ 30 min to airlock | walkback limit | walkback limit; no quick return to Earth |
| Spacewalks | ≈ 10 a year | a few per early landing | many over a long stay |

### Suitports and Rear-Entry Suits

Every trip through an airlock costs gas and time and brings dust inside. A **suitport** avoids this: the suit is stored outside the habitat or rover with its rear hatch sealed against a port in the wall, and the crew member climbs in through the back and detaches. NASA tested the idea on its Space Exploration Vehicle rover prototype and the Z-1 prototype suit in the early 2010s, and rear-entry suits such as the Orlan and AxEMU make it practical. Suitports would allow quick EVAs from pressurised rovers on long traverses, if the seals survive dust and repeated use.

::: ai
**AI for spacewalks**

AI is starting to support EVA in three ways. *Suit health monitoring:* models trained on suit telemetry can watch pressures, temperatures, fan speed and carbon dioxide and flag early signs of faults like the water intrusion of EVA 23, or a rising metabolic rate, faster than controllers watching many screens. *EVA planning and support:* optimisation tools can schedule tasks against consumables, lighting and walkback limits, and voice assistants in the helmet can read procedures, log progress and answer questions when the light-time delay to Mars — up to 22 minutes one way — makes ground support slow. *Robotic assistants:* free-flying robots and dexterous manipulators can fetch tools, hold lights and inspect worksites, and may eventually do routine external maintenance so that people go outside only when judgement and dexterity are needed. Chapters 45 and 46 describe these systems and how they can be verified for human spaceflight.
:::

::: key
**Key takeaways**

- A spacesuit is a one-person spacecraft: a pressure garment (bladder, restraint, thermal and micrometeoroid layers) plus a portable life support system that supplies oxygen, removes carbon dioxide and rejects heat.
- Suits run at 26–57 kPa: about 25 kPa of pure oxygen matches sea-level breathing, and lower pressure means more mobility, because the moment to bend a pressurised joint grows as $p r^3$.
- Constant-volume joints, bearings, hard torsos and custom gloves make work possible; hands and shoulders still suffer most.
- The PLSS of every suit since Apollo recirculates oxygen through a CO₂ scrubber and cools the astronaut by sublimating or evaporating water: about 0.38 kg of water per hour for 300 W.
- Moving from sea-level air to a 29.6 kPa suit gives a tissue ratio R = 2.70; oxygen prebreathe, exercise or a reduced-pressure campout brings it to about 1.65. Variable-pressure suits and low-pressure, oxygen-rich habitats can remove prebreathe altogether.
- EVA success depends on operations: tethers, restraints, neutral-buoyancy training, paced timelines and a 30-minute get-back or walkback reserve.
- In October 2026 the ISS still relied on suits designed in the 1970s; Axiom's AxEMU was in qualification for a first flight in 2027 and a landing planned for 2028, with little schedule margin.
:::
