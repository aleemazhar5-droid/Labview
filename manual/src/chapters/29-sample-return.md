title: Sample-Return Missions
hero: ch29
abstract: The most powerful instruments for studying another world are not on spacecraft but in laboratories on Earth. Sample-return missions bring pieces of the Moon, asteroids, a comet and even the solar wind home, where they can be dated, dissected atom by atom and preserved for scientists not yet born. This chapter explains how samples are collected — by drill, scoop, gas jet, bullet or aerogel — how they are launched off other worlds and carried back through Earth's atmosphere at up to 13 km/s, and how they are kept pristine. It reviews every sample-return mission from Apollo to OSIRIS-REx and Chang'e 6, what their samples have revealed, and the missions under way in 2026, including China's Tianwen-2 at the asteroid Kamoʻoalewa and the uncertain future of Mars Sample Return.
---

Flight instruments must be small, light, low-powered and able to survive launch. Laboratories on Earth have none of these limits. They can measure the age of a rock to within a few million years from the decay of radioactive isotopes, image individual atoms with transmission electron microscopes, analyse organic molecules at concentrations of parts per billion, and probe grains with synchrotron X-rays. Above all, samples can be **kept**: techniques that do not yet exist can be applied decades later. In 2022, NASA opened a tube of lunar soil that Apollo 17 astronauts had sealed in a vacuum on the Moon in 1972, so that it could be studied with instruments invented since.

| Capability | Spacecraft instrument | Laboratory on Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Radiometric age | Rough estimates only (one experimental dating on Curiosity) | Precise ages from many isotope systems (U–Pb, Rb–Sr, Ar–Ar, Sm–Nd) |
| Spatial resolution | ≈ 0.1 mm (PIXL on Perseverance) | Below 1 nm (electron microscopes, atom probes) |
| Organic analysis | A few target molecules | Thousands of compounds; chirality; isotopes of individual molecules |
| Number of techniques | A few, fixed at launch | Hundreds, improving every year |
| Re-analysis | Impossible once the mission ends | Samples curated for future generations |

## Sample-Return Missions So Far

Nine missions or programmes have returned extraterrestrial material to Earth (@fig:returnmasses). Their returns range from 382 kg of lunar rock to less than a milligram of comet dust and a fraction of a milligram of solar-wind atoms.

![Mass of extraterrestrial material returned by each mission (log scale).](figures/out/ch29_masses.svg){#fig:returnmasses width=94%}

### The Moon

The six **Apollo** landings (1969–72) brought back 382 kg of rocks, soil and cores — 2 196 samples in all — collected by astronauts trained in field geology (@fig:schmitt). Three robotic Soviet landers, **Luna 16, 20 and 24** (1970–76), returned a total of 326 g, including a 2 m drill core. Their analyses showed that the Moon formed hot, with a global magma ocean whose floating crystals created the bright highland crust, and supported the theory that it formed from debris thrown out when a Mars-sized body collided with the young Earth. Lunar samples also calibrated the **crater-counting chronology** used to estimate the ages of surfaces throughout the Solar System.

![Apollo 17 geologist-astronaut Harrison Schmitt beside a large split boulder at Taurus–Littrow in December 1972.](figures/photos/ch29_schmitt.jpg){#fig:schmitt width=56%}

After 44 years, China resumed lunar sample return. **Chang'e 5** (November–December 2020) landed in Oceanus Procellarum, drilled and scooped 1 731 g of soil, launched it into lunar orbit in an ascent stage that docked automatically with the orbiter — the first robotic rendezvous in lunar orbit — and returned it to Inner Mongolia using a "skip" re-entry that bounced off the upper atmosphere to reduce heating. Its basalts are only about 2.0 billion years old, showing that the Moon was volcanically active far later than Apollo samples had suggested. **Chang'e 6** (May–June 2024) returned 1 935 g from the Apollo crater inside the South Pole–Aitken basin, the first samples from the Moon's far side, relaying its signals through the Queqiao-2 satellite. They include basalts about 2.8 billion years old and fragments from volcanism 4.2 billion years ago, and suggest that the far-side mantle was drier than the near side's.

### A Comet and the Sun

NASA's **Stardust** flew 236 km from the nucleus of comet Wild 2 in January 2004 at 6.1 km/s and captured thousands of dust particles in **aerogel** — a silica foam less than 1 % as dense as glass — which slowed them over a few millimetres without vaporising them (@fig:aerogel). Its capsule re-entered Earth's atmosphere in January 2006 at 12.9 km/s, the fastest of any human-made object, protected by the first PICA (phenolic-impregnated carbon ablator) heat shield. The comet dust contained the amino acid glycine and, surprisingly, high-temperature minerals that could only have formed close to the young Sun — proof that material was mixed across the whole protoplanetary disk before the comets formed.

NASA's **Genesis** spent 27 months at the Sun–Earth L1 point (2001–04) exposing wafers of ultra-pure silicon, sapphire, gold and diamond to the solar wind, collecting about 0.4 mg of solar atoms. On its return in September 2004 its parachute failed to open — an acceleration sensor had been installed upside down — and the capsule hit the Utah desert at more than 300 km/h. Many collectors survived, and their analysis showed that the Sun contains a higher proportion of the oxygen isotope ¹⁶O than Earth, Moon and Mars, a clue to how the planets formed.

### Asteroids

Japan's **Hayabusa** survived failed reaction wheels, a fuel leak, seven weeks without contact and failing ion engines to return about 1 500 tiny grains of the asteroid Itokawa in June 2010. They proved that the most common type of asteroid (S-type) is the source of the most common meteorites (ordinary chondrites), linking telescope spectra with laboratory samples.

**Hayabusa2** reached the 900 m carbon-rich asteroid **Ryugu** in June 2018. It released two hopping rovers and the MASCOT lander, touched down to collect surface grains in February 2019, then fired a 2 kg copper projectile into the asteroid at 2 km/s to blast an artificial crater, and in July 2019 touched down beside it to collect material excavated from below the surface. Its capsule landed at Woomera, Australia, in December 2020 with 5.4 g of Ryugu. The samples are among the most primitive materials ever studied — chemically similar to the rare CI chondrite meteorites and to the composition of the Sun, but altered by liquid water — and contain more than 20 amino acids and the RNA nucleobase uracil. The spacecraft continues: it flew past the asteroid Torifune (2001 CC21) in July 2026 and will rendezvous with the small, fast-spinning asteroid 1998 KY26 in 2031.

NASA's **OSIRIS-REx** reached the 490 m asteroid **Bennu** in December 2018 and found a surface far rougher than expected, covered in boulders (@fig:bennu), and occasionally ejecting small particles into space. On 20 October 2020 it descended to the site called Nightingale and made a **touch-and-go (TAG)** contact lasting about six seconds: its sampler head released a burst of nitrogen gas that blew loose material into a collection chamber. The head sank almost half a metre into the surface, which turned out to behave like a pit of plastic balls; had the spacecraft not fired its thrusters to back away, it would have sunk further into the asteroid. So much material was collected that rocks wedged the sampler's flap open and particles leaked out, and the team stowed the sample early.

![Bennu's northern hemisphere seen by OSIRIS-REx in 2019. The asteroid's surface is covered in boulders, making a safe sampling site hard to find.](figures/photos/ch29_bennu.jpg){#fig:bennu width=88%}

The capsule landed in Utah on 24 September 2023 (@fig:capsule) with 121.6 g — twice the mission requirement. Two of the 35 fasteners on the sampler head resisted removal, and the curation team had to design new tools before they could open it fully in January 2024 (@fig:tagsam). In January 2025 scientists reported that the Bennu samples contain 14 of the 20 amino acids used by life on Earth, all five nucleobases of DNA and RNA, abundant ammonia, and a complete sequence of eleven salt minerals that crystallised as salty water evaporated on Bennu's parent body. The spacecraft, renamed **OSIRIS-APEX**, will reach the asteroid Apophis just after its close pass by Earth in April 2029.

## Engineering a Sample Return

A sample-return mission must do everything a lander does — and then reverse it: collect material, leave the surface, travel home and survive a high-speed entry into Earth's atmosphere, all without contaminating the sample.

### Collecting

The method depends on the target (@fig:techniques). On the Moon, drills and scoops on robotic arms collect regolith and cores. On small asteroids with almost no gravity, a spacecraft cannot stand on the surface and push, so **touch-and-go** techniques are used: Hayabusa's sampler horn touches the surface for about a second while a 5 g tantalum bullet fired at 300 m/s throws fragments up into a catcher, and OSIRIS-REx's TAGSAM used nitrogen gas to stir the regolith into its collector. Stardust captured dust during a fast flyby in aerogel, and Genesis simply exposed collectors to the solar wind.

![Sample-collection techniques.](figures/out/ch29_techniques.svg){#fig:techniques width=100%}

Missions must also confirm what they have collected. OSIRIS-REx planned to measure its sample by spinning the spacecraft and measuring the change in its moment of inertia; when it found it had collected too much, it instead photographed the sampler head and stowed it quickly to minimise losses.

### Leaving the Surface

Lifting a sample back into orbit requires a rocket — a small one on the Moon, a substantial one on Mars, and almost nothing on a small asteroid, where a gentle push exceeds the escape speed (@fig:ascent). Chang'e 5 and 6 used ascent stages that docked with the orbiter in lunar orbit and transferred the sample container, the same technique Apollo used with crews. For Mars, a **Mars Ascent Vehicle** must reach about 4.2 km/s, including losses, while surviving months on the surface at Martian temperatures. NASA's design was a two-stage solid rocket of about 400 kg lifting a 16 kg sample container — the first launch from another planet.

![Leaving the surface. (a) Ascent Δv to orbit, from a gentle push on an asteroid to 4.2 km/s on Mars. (b) Propellant fraction needed for two classes of propellant.](figures/out/ch29_ascent.svg){#fig:ascent width=98%}

::: example
**Example: sizing a Mars ascent vehicle**

The ascent vehicle must deliver Δv = 4.2 km/s using solid motors with $I_{sp}$ = 290 s ($v_e$ = 2 844 m/s). Assume each stage's structure, nozzle and casing weigh 12 % of the stage's loaded mass (structural ratio ε = 0.12).

1. **Single stage.** Mass ratio $R = e^{4\,200/2\,844}$ = 4.38, so 77 % of the lift-off mass is propellant. The payload fraction is $(1/R - ε)/(1-ε)$ = (0.228 − 0.12)/0.88 = **12.3 %**.
2. **Two equal stages,** each providing 2.1 km/s: $R$ = 2.09 per stage; payload fraction per stage (0.478 − 0.12)/0.88 = 0.407; overall 0.407² = **16.5 %**.
3. Staging increases the payload by about a third and leaves margin for the real penalties — guidance, thermal protection, the launch structure and the need to fire after months in the cold — which in NASA's design reduced the payload fraction to about 4 %.
4. For comparison, a lunar ascent needs only 1.9 km/s: with storable propellants ($I_{sp}$ ≈ 315 s), propellant is 46 % of the ascent stage's mass.
:::

### Coming Home

From the Moon, the return trip takes a few days; from asteroids and Mars, a year or more. Shortly before arrival the spacecraft releases a small **sample-return capsule** on a trajectory that intercepts Earth directly, and then diverts itself to fly past. The capsule enters at 11–13 km/s, much faster than a spacecraft returning from Earth orbit at 7.8 km/s. Because the heating rate rises roughly as the cube of the speed, a capsule entering at 12.4 km/s (OSIRIS-REx) experiences about four times the peak heat flux of one returning from low Earth orbit. Capsules use ablative heat shields of PICA or carbon phenolic, deploy parachutes at a few kilometres' altitude, and land in remote, closely controlled areas — the US Air Force's Utah Test and Training Range, the Woomera Range in South Australia, or Inner Mongolia in China — where recovery teams wait with helicopters and clean containers.

### Keeping Samples Pristine

A sample is only as valuable as its purity. Contamination by terrestrial organics, water or even fingerprints can ruin the search for prebiotic chemistry. Spacecraft that touch samples are built from a short list of approved, well-characterised materials; **witness plates** fly alongside the samples to record any contamination; capsules are purged with dry nitrogen immediately after landing; and the samples are opened and processed in glove boxes filled with pure nitrogen at dedicated **curation facilities**, such as NASA's at the Johnson Space Center (@fig:curation) and JAXA's at Sagamihara. Fractions are shared among agencies: NASA received 10 % of the Ryugu sample, and JAXA and the Canadian Space Agency received portions of Bennu.

![Scientists in clean-room suits examining asteroid Ryugu particles at NASA's Johnson Space Center, part of the sample exchanged with JAXA.](figures/photos/ch29_ryugu.jpg){#fig:curation width=62%}

### Planetary Protection for Returned Samples

International planetary protection policy places samples from the Moon, comets and most asteroids in the **unrestricted Earth return** category: they are not thought to pose any biological risk. Samples from Mars, Europa or Enceladus — places that might harbour life — fall into **restricted Earth return** (Category V): the mission must "break the chain of contact" with the target body, so that nothing that has touched Mars can reach Earth's environment outside a sealed container, and the samples must be opened in a high-containment receiving facility comparable to a biosafety-level-4 laboratory until they are shown to be safe. The capsule designed for Mars Sample Return was to land without a parachute — a parachute that might fail was considered a greater risk than a deliberate impact onto a crushable, energy-absorbing structure designed to keep the sample container intact (@fig:ees).

![Artist's concept of the Earth Entry System designed for Mars Sample Return approaching Earth.](figures/photos/ch29_ees.jpg){#fig:ees width=64%}

## Mars Sample Return and the Next Decade

**Mars Sample Return (MSR)** was planned as a NASA–ESA campaign to bring Perseverance's sealed tubes to Earth (@fig:msr). A Sample Retrieval Lander would receive the tubes and launch them into Mars orbit on the Mars Ascent Vehicle; ESA's Earth Return Orbiter would capture the container and carry it home. As the design matured, the estimated cost rose to about US$ 8–11 billion and the return date slipped to the late 2030s or 2040. NASA studied cheaper alternatives in 2024–25, but the US budget for fiscal year 2026 provided no money for the existing programme. In October 2026 the samples, including one with a potential biosignature (Chapter 28), remain on Mars without a funded plan to retrieve them.

![The NASA–ESA Mars Sample Return architecture and China's simpler Tianwen-3 approach.](figures/out/ch29_msr.svg){#fig:msr width=98%}

China plans to reach the goal first. **Tianwen-3**, to be launched on two Long March 5 rockets in 2028, will land, drill and scoop at its own site, launch the samples to a waiting orbiter and return them to Earth in 2031. Other sample-return missions under way or planned include:

- **Tianwen-2** (CNSA): launched in May 2025, it arrived at the small near-Earth asteroid **Kamoʻoalewa**, a quasi-satellite of Earth that spins once every 28 minutes and may be a fragment of the Moon, in mid-2026. It is mapping the asteroid before sampling, and is due to return its capsule to Earth in late 2027 before continuing to the main-belt comet 311P.
- **MMX** (JAXA): scheduled for launch in October 2026 on an H3 rocket, it will orbit Mars in 2027, land on Phobos with the French–German IDEFIX rover in 2029, collect at least 10 g of Phobos regolith with a coring sampler and pneumatic sampler, and return them in 2031.
- **Chandrayaan-4** (ISRO): approved in 2024, it will be launched in two parts on two LVM-3 rockets, its five modules docking in Earth and lunar orbit to return about 3 kg from near the south pole, around 2027–28.
- **Artemis**: astronauts landing near the lunar south pole from the late 2020s will collect samples from the oldest and most volatile-rich regions of the Moon (Chapters 31 and 33).

::: ai
**AI in sample-return missions**

Touch-and-go sampling depends on autonomous navigation: OSIRIS-REx used **Natural Feature Tracking**, comparing images taken during its descent with an onboard catalogue of Bennu's boulders to update its trajectory and to abort automatically if it was heading for a hazard, and Hayabusa2 navigated using target markers it dropped on the surface. On Earth, machine learning segments X-ray computed-tomography scans of thousands of particles to classify minerals and find rare grains without destroying them, and helps curators track every fragment as samples are subdivided among hundreds of laboratories (Chapter 46).
:::

::: key
**Key takeaways**

- Laboratories on Earth can date, image and analyse samples far beyond the capability of flight instruments, and curated samples serve future generations.
- Nine missions or programmes have returned extraterrestrial material: Apollo, Luna, Stardust, Genesis, Hayabusa, Hayabusa2, OSIRIS-REx, Chang'e 5 and Chang'e 6.
- Collection techniques are matched to gravity: drills and scoops on the Moon, touch-and-go with gas or projectiles on asteroids, aerogel during a comet flyby.
- Leaving the surface costs 1.9 km/s from the Moon and 4.2 km/s from Mars; return capsules enter Earth's atmosphere at 11–13 km/s and need ablative heat shields.
- Samples revealed the Moon's violent origin and late volcanism, the mixing of the early Solar System, and abundant prebiotic molecules on carbon-rich asteroids.
- Mars samples require "restricted Earth return". NASA's Mars Sample Return lost its funding in 2026, while China's Tianwen-3 aims to return Martian samples in 2031; Tianwen-2, MMX and Chandrayaan-4 are under way or in preparation.
:::
