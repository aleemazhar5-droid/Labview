---
title: Glossary
abstract: Plain-language definitions of about 300 technical terms used in this manual, from ablator to zenith, covering orbital mechanics, spacecraft engineering, launch, remote sensing, communications, the space environment, exploration, human spaceflight and artificial intelligence, with pointers to the chapters where each is explained.
---

Terms in *italics* within a definition have their own entry. The number in brackets is the chapter where the term is explained most fully; letters refer to appendices. Acronyms are listed in Appendix G.

## A

::: glossary
**Ablator.** A heat-shield material that protects a vehicle during atmospheric entry by charring, melting or vaporising, carrying heat away with the lost material. Examples are PICA and Avcoat (10, 27).

**Absorptance (α).** The fraction of incident radiation, usually sunlight, that a surface absorbs. With *emittance* it sets a surface's equilibrium temperature (5).

**Active debris removal.** Deliberately capturing and de-orbiting or re-orbiting a defunct satellite or rocket body with another spacecraft (21, 40).

**Adaptive coding and modulation.** Changing a link's modulation and error-correcting code from moment to moment to match the received signal quality, so throughput stays high in clear weather and the link survives rain fades (7).

**Aerobraking.** Using repeated passes through the upper atmosphere of a planet to lower an orbit gradually, saving propellant (25).

**Aerocapture.** Using a single deep pass through an atmosphere to slow a spacecraft from a hyperbolic approach into orbit (25).

**Airy disc.** The bright central spot of the diffraction pattern produced by a circular aperture; its angular radius, 1.22 λ/*D*, sets the resolution limit of a telescope (14, 38).

**Albedo.** The fraction of incoming sunlight that a body or surface reflects. Earth's average is about 0.3 (2, 13).

**Analysis-ready data.** Satellite data already corrected and formatted (for example to surface reflectance on a standard grid) so that users can analyse it without further processing (13, 46).

**Anomaly.** (1) In orbital mechanics, an angle giving a body's position along its orbit — true, eccentric or mean anomaly (3). (2) In operations, any unexpected behaviour of a spacecraft (49).

**Antenna gain.** How strongly an antenna concentrates power in its main direction compared with an isotropic antenna, usually in dBi (7).

**Aperture synthesis.** Combining signals from separated antennas or positions to obtain the resolution of a much larger aperture — the principle behind *synthetic aperture radar* and radio interferometers (15).

**Apoapsis / periapsis.** The farthest and nearest points of an orbit from the central body; called apogee and perigee for Earth, aphelion and perihelion for the Sun (3).

**Argument of periapsis (ω).** The angle, in the orbital plane, from the *ascending node* to periapsis (3).

**Artemis Accords.** Non-binding principles for civil exploration of the Moon and beyond, led by the United States; 76 nations had signed by late September 2026 (41).

**Artificial intelligence (AI).** Computer systems that perform tasks normally requiring human intelligence, such as perception, planning or language. Most current space applications use *machine learning* (43).

**Ascending node.** The point where an orbit crosses the equatorial plane going north. Its longitude is the *right ascension of the ascending node* (3).

**Astronomical unit (au).** The mean Earth–Sun distance, fixed at exactly 149 597 870 700 m (A).

**Atomic oxygen.** Single oxygen atoms, the main constituent of the atmosphere at 200–600 km, which erode polymers and some coatings on spacecraft (2).

**Attitude.** The orientation of a spacecraft in space. The attitude determination and control subsystem measures and controls it (6).

**Autonomy.** The ability of a spacecraft or ground system to make decisions and act without human intervention. ECSS defines levels E1 (ground control) to E4 (goal-oriented on-board operations) (48).
:::

## B

::: glossary
**Ballistic coefficient (β).** Mass divided by the product of drag coefficient and reference area, *m*/(*C*~D~*A*). A high ballistic coefficient means the vehicle slows down less in the atmosphere (10, 21).

**Ballistic missile.** A missile that is powered only during the first part of its flight and then follows a free-fall trajectory, often reaching space (20).

**Bandwidth.** The range of frequencies a signal occupies or a system can pass, in hertz. It limits data rate and radar range resolution (7, 15).

**Barycentre.** The centre of mass of two or more bodies, around which they both orbit (3).

**Beamforming.** Steering and shaping an antenna's beam electronically by adjusting the phase and amplitude of many elements of a *phased array* (17).

**Bipropellant.** A rocket propellant system using separate fuel and oxidiser, such as hydrazine and nitrogen tetroxide or methane and liquid oxygen (6, 35).

**Blackbody.** An ideal object that absorbs all radiation falling on it and emits radiation according to *Planck's law* (13).

**Body-mounted array.** Solar cells fixed to a spacecraft's outer surfaces rather than on deployable wings (5).

**Boil-off.** The loss of cryogenic propellant by evaporation due to heat leaking into a tank (35, 40).

**Bond albedo.** The fraction of total incident solar power reflected by a planet in all directions and at all wavelengths (C).

**Bus (satellite bus).** The part of a satellite that provides structure, power, thermal control, attitude control, propulsion and communications to support the *payload* (12).
:::

## C

::: glossary
**C3 (characteristic energy).** Twice the specific orbital energy of a departure hyperbola, equal to the square of the *hyperbolic excess speed*; a launch vehicle's capability is quoted as payload mass versus C3 (24).

**Cadence (revisit).** How often a satellite or constellation can observe the same place (11, 14).

**Capsule.** A blunt, wingless re-entry vehicle, such as Soyuz, Dragon or Orion (31).

**Catalogue (space object catalogue).** The database of tracked objects in orbit with their orbital elements, maintained by the US Space Force and others (21).

**Characteristic velocity (c\*).** A measure of combustion performance in a rocket chamber: chamber pressure times throat area divided by mass flow rate (35).

**Chemical propulsion.** Rocket propulsion in which energy comes from chemical reaction of the propellants (35).

**Clean room.** A room in which airborne particles are controlled to protect sensitive hardware, classified by particle count (ISO class) (4).

**Coherent.** Describing waves that keep a fixed phase relationship. Radar and interferometric processing depend on coherent signals (15).

**Collision probability (Pc).** The estimated probability that two objects will collide during a close approach, used to decide whether to manoeuvre (21).

**Commissioning.** The period after launch in which a spacecraft's systems and instruments are switched on, checked and calibrated before routine operations (4).

**Conjunction.** A predicted close approach between two space objects (21).

**Constellation.** A group of satellites working together, arranged in planes and phases to provide coverage (11).

**Convolutional neural network (CNN).** A *neural network* that applies learned filters across an image; the standard tool for image classification and detection (44).

**Coronagraph.** An instrument that blocks the light of a star inside a telescope so faint nearby objects, such as planets, can be seen (38).

**Coronal mass ejection (CME).** A huge cloud of magnetised plasma thrown out from the Sun, which can cause a *geomagnetic storm* when it reaches Earth (22).

**Cosmic rays.** Very energetic charged particles, mostly protons and heavier nuclei, from outside the Solar System (*galactic cosmic rays*) or from the Sun (2, 22).

**Cross-range.** How far sideways from its original ground track a re-entry vehicle can steer, which depends on its *lift-to-drag ratio* (10).

**Cryocooler.** A mechanical refrigerator that cools detectors or instruments to cryogenic temperatures, as on JWST's MIRI instrument (38).

**Cryogenic propellant.** A propellant stored as a very cold liquid — liquid hydrogen (20 K), liquid oxygen (90 K) or liquid methane (112 K) (35).

**CubeSat.** A standard small satellite built from 10 cm units (1U); 3U, 6U and 12U are common sizes (11).
:::

## D

::: glossary
**Data assimilation.** Combining observations with a numerical model to produce the best estimate of the state of a system, such as the atmosphere for a weather forecast (16, 47).

**Decibel (dB).** Ten times the base-10 logarithm of a power ratio (A, D).

**Deep learning.** *Machine learning* with neural networks of many layers (44).

**Deep Space Network.** NASA's three ground stations, at Goldstone, Madrid and Canberra, about 120° apart in longitude, which communicate with deep-space missions (7, 24).

**Delta-v (Δv).** The change in velocity a manoeuvre or mission requires; the currency of mission design (3).

**Depth of discharge.** The fraction of a battery's capacity used in a discharge cycle; lower depth gives longer life (5).

**Diffraction limit.** The finest angular detail an optical system can resolve, set by the wavelength divided by the aperture diameter (14, 38).

**Digital twin.** A continuously updated computer model of a physical system used to predict its behaviour and test operations (49, 52).

**Direct-to-device.** Satellite service to unmodified mobile phones, using terrestrial mobile spectrum (17).

**Dispersion (launch).** The spread of possible trajectories or impact points around the nominal, used in range safety (37).

**Distribution shift.** A change between the data on which an AI model was trained and the data it meets in operation, often degrading performance (43, 53).

**Docking / berthing.** Joining two spacecraft. In docking the active vehicle flies into contact; in berthing a robotic arm moves one vehicle onto the other's port (31, 32).

**Doppler shift.** The change in received frequency caused by relative motion between transmitter and receiver, used for navigation and radar (7, 18).

**Dose (radiation).** The energy deposited by radiation per kilogram of material (gray); weighted by biological effect it becomes equivalent dose (sievert) (2, 30).

**Downlink / uplink.** Transmission from space to ground / from ground to space (7).

**Drag.** The aerodynamic force opposing motion through an atmosphere; in low orbit it slowly lowers satellites (2, 21).
:::

## E

::: glossary
**Earth observation.** Gathering information about the Earth's surface, oceans and atmosphere from space (13).

**Eccentricity (e).** A number describing the shape of an orbit: 0 for a circle, between 0 and 1 for an ellipse, 1 for a parabola and above 1 for a hyperbola (3).

**Ecliptic.** The plane of the Earth's orbit around the Sun (3).

**EIRP (effective isotropic radiated power).** The transmitter power multiplied by the antenna gain — the power an isotropic antenna would need to give the same signal in the main beam (7).

**Electric propulsion.** Propulsion that accelerates propellant electrically — ion, Hall-effect, arcjet or electrospray thrusters — giving high *specific impulse* at low thrust (6, 42).

**Electrolysis.** Splitting a compound with electricity, for example water into hydrogen and oxygen for propellant or breathing (30, 41).

**Emittance (ε).** How effectively a surface radiates heat compared with a *blackbody* (5).

**Engineering model.** A representative version of a spacecraft or unit built to verify design and test procedures, but not intended to fly (4).

**Entry, descent and landing (EDL).** The sequence that takes a spacecraft from the top of an atmosphere to the surface (27).

**Ephemeris.** A table or model of the positions of a body over time (3).

**Equatorial bulge (J2).** The flattening of the Earth, which makes orbital planes precess; described by the *J*~2~ coefficient (3).

**Escape velocity.** The speed needed at a given distance to leave a body's gravity without further propulsion, √(2μ/*r*) (3).

**Exhaust velocity (effective).** The speed at which a rocket's exhaust leaves relative to the vehicle, equal to *specific impulse* × *g*₀ (35).

**Exoplanet.** A planet orbiting a star other than the Sun (39).

**Extravehicular activity (EVA).** Any activity by an astronaut outside a spacecraft — a spacewalk or moonwalk (33).
:::

## F

::: glossary
**Fairing (payload fairing).** The aerodynamic nose cone that protects payloads during ascent and is jettisoned above the dense atmosphere (34).

**Fault detection, isolation and recovery (FDIR).** The on-board logic that detects faults, identifies the failed unit and switches to a safe configuration or a redundant unit (6, 48).

**Field of regard / field of view.** The region a sensor can be pointed at / the region it sees at one instant (13).

**Flyby.** A mission that passes a target body without orbiting or landing (26).

**Flight-path angle (γ).** The angle between a vehicle's velocity and the local horizontal (3, 10).

**Footprint.** The area on the ground covered by a satellite's antenna beam or sensor (11, 17).

**Formation flying.** Two or more spacecraft keeping a precise relative position, for example for interferometry (11).

**Foundation model.** A large *machine-learning* model trained on broad data, usually with *self-supervised learning*, that can be adapted to many tasks (44, 46).

**Free-space path loss.** The spreading loss of a radio signal with distance between isotropic antennas (7, D).

**Frozen orbit.** An orbit whose eccentricity and argument of perigee are chosen so that they stay nearly constant despite perturbations (11).
:::

## G

::: glossary
**Gain-to-noise temperature ratio (G/T).** The figure of merit of a receiving system: antenna gain divided by system noise temperature (7).

**Gateway (feeder link).** A ground station that connects a communication satellite to terrestrial networks (17).

**Geomagnetic storm.** A major disturbance of the Earth's magnetosphere caused by solar-wind structures, measured by indices such as Kp and Dst (22).

**Geostationary orbit (GEO).** A circular orbit 35 786 km above the equator with a period of one *sidereal day*, in which a satellite appears fixed in the sky (11).

**Geosynchronous orbit.** Any orbit with a period of one sidereal day; geostationary orbit is the equatorial, circular case (11).

**Gimbal.** A pivoted mount that lets an engine or instrument rotate about one or two axes; engine gimbals steer rockets (34).

**Gravity assist (swing-by).** Using a close pass of a planet to change a spacecraft's speed and direction relative to the Sun without propellant (24).

**Gravity gradient.** The variation of gravity across a body, which produces torques and tidal forces (6).

**Gravity loss.** The part of a rocket's Δ*v* spent fighting gravity during ascent rather than gaining speed (34).

**Ground sample distance (GSD).** The distance on the ground between the centres of adjacent pixels in an image (13, 14).

**Ground segment.** All the ground facilities — antennas, control centres, data processing — that operate a space system (7).

**Ground track.** The path on the Earth's surface directly beneath an orbiting satellite (3).
:::

## H

::: glossary
**Halo orbit.** A periodic three-dimensional orbit around a *Lagrange point*, used by JWST and many solar observatories (3, 38).

**Hall-effect thruster.** An *electric propulsion* device that traps electrons in a magnetic field to ionise and accelerate propellant, typically xenon or krypton (6, 42).

**Heat pipe.** A sealed tube containing a working fluid that moves heat by evaporation and condensation with very small temperature drop (5).

**Heliopause.** The boundary where the solar wind meets the interstellar medium, crossed by Voyager 1 in 2012 and Voyager 2 in 2018 (26).

**High-altitude platform station (HAPS).** An aircraft or airship that stays in the stratosphere, typically at about 20 km, for weeks or months (8).

**Highly elliptical orbit (HEO).** An orbit with high eccentricity, such as a Molniya or Tundra orbit, giving long dwell time over high latitudes (11, 20).

**Hohmann transfer.** The two-impulse transfer between coplanar circular orbits with the least Δ*v* (3, 24).

**Hydrazine.** N₂H₄, a storable propellant used alone over a catalyst (monopropellant) or with an oxidiser (6).

**Hyperbolic excess speed (v∞).** The speed a spacecraft has relative to a planet far outside its gravity well (24).

**Hypergolic.** Describing propellants that ignite spontaneously on contact, such as hydrazines with nitrogen tetroxide (35).

**Hyperspectral imaging.** Imaging in many (often hundreds of) narrow contiguous spectral bands, giving a spectrum for each pixel (13).
:::

## I

::: glossary
**Inclination (i).** The angle between an orbital plane and the reference plane, usually the Earth's equator (3).

**Inertial measurement unit (IMU).** A package of gyroscopes and accelerometers that measures rotation and acceleration for navigation (6, 34).

**In situ resource utilisation (ISRU).** Producing useful materials, such as oxygen, water or propellant, from local resources on the Moon, Mars or asteroids (41).

**Integration and test.** Assembling a spacecraft and verifying it by functional, environmental and end-to-end tests (4).

**Interferometric SAR (InSAR).** Comparing the phase of two radar images to measure topography or surface movement to millimetres (15).

**Interplanetary trajectory.** A path between planets, usually designed with *patched conics* and refined numerically (24).

**Ionosphere.** The ionised upper atmosphere, from about 60 km to more than 1 000 km, which reflects, delays and scatters radio waves (23).

**Isotropic radiator.** An ideal antenna radiating equally in all directions; the reference for *antenna gain* (7).
:::

## J–K

::: glossary
**Jitter.** Small, rapid unwanted motions of a pointing system, which blur images (6, 38).

**Kármán line.** The altitude of 100 km often used as the boundary of space (FAI); the US military and FAA use 50 miles (80 km) (1, 9).

**Kepler's laws.** Three laws of planetary motion: orbits are ellipses with the central body at one focus; a line from the central body sweeps equal areas in equal times; the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis (3).

**Kessler syndrome.** A cascade in which collisions between debris objects create more debris, making further collisions more likely (21).

**Kick stage.** A small upper stage that delivers payloads to their final orbits after separation from the main launch vehicle (34).
:::

## L

::: glossary
**Lagrange point.** One of five points in a two-body system where a small object can remain stationary relative to the two bodies (3, C).

**Lander.** A spacecraft that makes a controlled landing on a surface (27).

**Laser communication (optical communication).** Using modulated laser light instead of radio waves to send data, allowing higher rates with smaller terminals (7).

**Latency.** The delay between sending and receiving a signal or a response — about 0.25 s each way via GEO, about 25–40 ms round trip via low-orbit broadband (7, 17).

**Launch window.** The period during which a launch can reach the desired orbit or trajectory (24, 34).

**Lift-to-drag ratio (L/D).** The ratio of aerodynamic lift to drag; it sets how much a re-entry vehicle can steer and how gently it decelerates (10).

**Light-time.** The time light takes to travel between two points — 1.3 s to the Moon, 3–22 minutes to Mars (7, 24).

**Link budget.** An accounting of all gains and losses between a transmitter and receiver, used to show that a communication link closes with margin (7).

**Liquid rocket engine.** An engine burning liquid propellants fed from tanks by pressure or pumps (35).

**Long-duration balloon.** A scientific balloon that flies for weeks, typically circling Antarctica in summer (8).

**Low Earth orbit (LEO).** Orbits from about 160 km to 2 000 km altitude (11).

**Lunar south pole.** The region around the Moon's south pole, of interest because permanently shadowed craters may hold water ice (27, 41).
:::

## M

::: glossary
**Machine learning (ML).** Methods by which computers learn patterns from data instead of following explicitly programmed rules (43).

**Magnetometer.** An instrument that measures magnetic field strength and direction, used for attitude sensing and space science (6, 23).

**Magnetorquer.** An electromagnet that produces attitude control torques by interacting with the Earth's magnetic field (6).

**Magnetosphere.** The region around a planet dominated by its magnetic field, shaped by the solar wind into a long tail (23).

**Main-belt asteroid.** An asteroid orbiting between Mars and Jupiter (26, 41).

**Margin.** The difference between what a design can do and what is required, kept to cover uncertainty (4).

**Mass fraction (propellant mass fraction).** The fraction of a stage's mass that is propellant (34).

**Max-Q.** The point in ascent at which *dynamic pressure* is greatest (34).

**Medium Earth orbit (MEO).** Orbits between LEO and GEO, notably those of GNSS satellites at about 19 000–23 000 km (11, 18).

**Micrometeoroid.** A natural particle smaller than about 1 mm travelling at tens of km/s (2).

**Microgravity.** The condition of apparent weightlessness in free fall, with residual accelerations around a millionth of *g* (9, 30).

**Mission control centre.** The facility from which a mission is commanded and monitored (7).

**Model compression.** Reducing an AI model's size and computational cost — by *quantisation*, pruning or distillation — so that it can run on limited hardware such as spacecraft processors (45).

**Molniya orbit.** A highly elliptical, 12-hour orbit with 63.4° inclination, giving long dwell over high northern latitudes (11).

**Monopropellant.** A single propellant that decomposes over a catalyst to produce thrust, usually hydrazine (6).

**Multi-layer insulation (MLI).** A blanket of many thin, reflective layers separated by spacers, which greatly reduces radiative heat transfer (5).

**Multispectral imaging.** Imaging in several (typically 4–15) spectral bands (13).
:::

## N

::: glossary
**Near-Earth object (NEO).** An asteroid or comet whose orbit brings it within 1.3 au of the Sun (26).

**Near-rectilinear halo orbit (NRHO).** A highly elongated *halo orbit* around the Moon, planned for the Gateway station (32).

**Neural network.** A machine-learning model made of layers of simple connected units ("neurons") whose connection weights are learned from data (43, 44).

**Nodal precession (regression of the nodes).** The slow rotation of an orbital plane about the Earth's axis caused by the *equatorial bulge* (3).

**Noise temperature.** A way of expressing noise power as the temperature of an equivalent resistor (7).

**Nowcasting.** Forecasting weather for the next few hours, largely by extrapolating the latest observations (16, 47).

**Nuclear thermal propulsion.** Heating hydrogen in a nuclear reactor and expanding it through a nozzle, giving roughly twice the specific impulse of chemical rockets (42).
:::

## O

::: glossary
**Oberth effect.** A burn performed at high speed, deep in a gravity well, produces a greater change in orbital energy than the same burn at low speed (3, 24).

**Occultation.** The passing of one body in front of another; radio and GNSS signals passing through an atmosphere during occultation are used to measure its profile (16, 26).

**On-board processing.** Processing data on the spacecraft, for example to filter clouds or detect events, rather than downlinking raw data (12, 45).

**Optical depth.** A measure of how much a medium attenuates light; an optical depth of 1 transmits 37 % (13).

**Orbit determination.** Estimating a spacecraft's orbit from tracking measurements (3, 21).

**Orbital elements.** Six numbers that define an orbit and a position on it, such as semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination, right ascension of the ascending node, argument of periapsis and true anomaly (3).

**Orbital period.** The time to complete one orbit (3).

**Outgassing.** The release of gas from materials in vacuum, which can contaminate optics and cold surfaces (2, 38).

**Overfitting.** When a machine-learning model learns the details and noise of its training data so closely that it performs poorly on new data (43).
:::

## P

::: glossary
**Parking orbit.** A temporary orbit in which a spacecraft waits before a departure burn (24).

**Patched conics.** Approximating a trajectory as a sequence of two-body conic sections, each inside one body's *sphere of influence* (24).

**Payload.** The part of a spacecraft or launch vehicle that performs the mission — instruments, transponders or passengers (12, 34).

**Perturbation.** Any force other than the central body's point-mass gravity that alters an orbit — *J*₂, drag, third bodies, solar radiation pressure (3).

**Phased array.** An antenna made of many elements whose relative phases are controlled to steer the beam electronically (17).

**Physics-informed machine learning.** Machine-learning models constrained or guided by known physical laws (44).

**Pixel.** One element of a digital image or detector array (13).

**Planck's law.** The equation giving the spectrum of radiation emitted by a *blackbody* at a given temperature (13, B).

**Planetary protection.** Measures to prevent biological contamination of other worlds by Earth organisms, and of Earth by returned samples (24, 29).

**Plasma.** An ionised gas of free electrons and ions; most of the matter in space is plasma (23).

**Polar orbit.** An orbit with inclination near 90°, passing over both poles (11).

**Pose estimation.** Determining the position and orientation of an object, such as a target spacecraft, from sensor data (48).

**Precession.** A slow rotation of the axis of a spinning body or of an orbital plane (3, 6).

**Pressure-fed / pump-fed.** Engine feed systems in which propellant is pushed into the chamber by tank pressure or by turbopumps (35).

**Primary mirror.** The main light-collecting mirror of a reflecting telescope (38).

**Proximity operations.** Manoeuvring close to another spacecraft for inspection, docking or servicing (40).

**Push-broom scanner.** An imager with a line of detectors across the ground track that builds an image as the satellite moves forward (13).
:::

## Q–R

::: glossary
**Quantisation (AI).** Representing a model's weights and activations with fewer bits, for example 8-bit integers instead of 32-bit floating point (45).

**Quaternion.** A four-number representation of attitude that avoids the singularities of Euler angles (6).

**Radar cross-section (RCS).** The effective area that an object presents to a radar, in m² or dBsm (15, 21).

**Radiation belts (Van Allen belts).** Regions of trapped energetic electrons and protons in the Earth's magnetosphere (2, 23).

**Radiator.** A surface that rejects waste heat to space by thermal radiation (5).

**Radioisotope thermoelectric generator (RTG).** A power source that converts heat from radioactive decay of plutonium-238 into electricity (5, 26).

**Reaction wheel.** A spinning flywheel whose speed is changed by a motor to exchange angular momentum with the spacecraft and control its attitude (6).

**Re-entry.** The return of a vehicle into the atmosphere from space (10).

**Regolith.** The layer of loose, broken rock and dust covering a planetary surface (27, 41).

**Reinforcement learning.** Machine learning in which an agent learns a policy by trial and error, guided by rewards (43, 48).

**Relay satellite.** A satellite that forwards communications between other spacecraft and the ground, such as TDRS or Queqiao (7).

**Remote sensing.** Acquiring information about an object without physical contact, usually by measuring electromagnetic radiation (13).

**Rendezvous.** Bringing two spacecraft to the same orbit and position at the same time (31, 40).

**Resolution.** The ability to distinguish detail: spatial, spectral, radiometric or temporal (13).

**Retrograde orbit.** An orbit in the opposite direction to the central body's rotation (inclination above 90°) (3).

**Reusability.** Recovering and reflying launch vehicle stages, fairings or spacecraft (34, 36).

**Revisit time.** The time between successive observations of the same location (11).

**Right ascension of the ascending node (RAAN, Ω).** The angle from the vernal equinox direction to the *ascending node*, measured in the equatorial plane (3).

**Rover.** A mobile robotic vehicle that explores a planetary surface (28).
:::

## S

::: glossary
**Safe mode.** A minimal, stable spacecraft configuration entered automatically after a fault, waiting for ground instructions (6, 48).

**Sample return.** A mission that collects material from another body and brings it to Earth (29).

**Scale height.** The altitude change over which atmospheric pressure or density falls by a factor of *e* (2.718) (2).

**Scintillation.** Rapid fluctuations of a signal's amplitude and phase caused by irregularities in the medium it passes through, such as the ionosphere (23).

**Scramjet.** A supersonic-combustion ramjet, an air-breathing engine for hypersonic flight (42).

**Segmented mirror.** A large mirror made of smaller segments controlled to act as one surface, as on JWST (38).

**Self-supervised learning.** Machine learning in which a model learns from unlabelled data by predicting hidden parts of its input (44).

**Semi-major axis (a).** Half the longest diameter of an ellipse; it determines an orbit's period and energy (3).

**Sensor fusion.** Combining data from different sensors to obtain better estimates than any single sensor (6, 48).

**Servicing (on-orbit).** Inspecting, repairing, refuelling or upgrading a spacecraft in orbit (40).

**Sidereal day.** The Earth's rotation period relative to the stars, 23 h 56 min 4.1 s (3, A).

**Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).** The ratio of signal power to noise power (7, 13).

**Signals intelligence (SIGINT).** Intelligence obtained by intercepting communications and electronic emissions (19).

**Single-event effect.** A malfunction caused by one energetic particle striking a sensitive part of a microcircuit — an upset (bit flip), latch-up or burnout (2).

**Small satellite.** A satellite of less than about 500 kg, including minisatellites, microsatellites, nanosatellites and *CubeSats* (11).

**Solar array.** An assembly of photovoltaic cells that converts sunlight into electricity (5).

**Solar cycle.** The roughly 11-year cycle of solar activity, measured by sunspot number (22).

**Solar flare.** A sudden release of energy in the Sun's atmosphere, producing X-rays and often energetic particles (22).

**Solar sail.** A large, thin reflective membrane propelled by the pressure of sunlight (42).

**Solar wind.** The continuous outflow of plasma from the Sun at 300–800 km/s (22).

**Sounding rocket.** A rocket that carries instruments on a suborbital flight, typically for 5–20 minutes above 100 km (9).

**Space situational awareness (SSA) / space domain awareness.** Knowledge of the location and behaviour of objects in space, and of the environment they operate in (21).

**Space weather.** Conditions in space, driven mainly by the Sun, that affect technology and human health (22).

**Specific impulse (Isp).** Thrust per unit weight flow of propellant, in seconds — a measure of propellant efficiency (35).

**Sphere of influence.** The region around a planet within which its gravity dominates for trajectory calculations (24, C).

**Spin stabilisation.** Keeping a spacecraft's attitude stable by spinning it (6).

**Staging.** Discarding empty tanks and engines during ascent so that the remaining vehicle is lighter (34).

**Star tracker.** A camera that identifies star patterns to determine a spacecraft's attitude to arc-second accuracy (6).

**Starshade.** A large, flower-shaped screen flown in front of a telescope to block a star's light for exoplanet imaging (38).

**Station-keeping.** Small manoeuvres that keep a satellite in its assigned orbit or slot (11).

**Stratosphere.** The atmospheric layer from about 11–15 km to 50 km, where temperature rises with height (2, 8).

**Suborbital flight.** A flight that reaches space but does not have enough speed to complete an orbit (9).

**Sun-synchronous orbit.** A near-polar orbit whose plane precesses once a year, so the satellite crosses each latitude at the same local solar time (11).

**Supervised learning.** Machine learning from examples labelled with the correct answer (43).

**Surrogate model (emulator).** A fast approximate model, often learned from data, that stands in for a slow physical simulation (44).

**Swath.** The width of the strip of ground imaged by a satellite on each pass (13).

**Synodic period.** The time between successive identical alignments of two orbiting bodies, such as Earth and Mars (24, C).

**Synthetic aperture radar (SAR).** An imaging radar that uses the motion of the platform to synthesise a long antenna, giving fine resolution day and night through clouds (15).
:::

## T

::: glossary
**Telemetry.** Measurements sent from a spacecraft to the ground describing its health and status (7).

**Telecommand.** Instructions sent from the ground to a spacecraft (7).

**Terrain-relative navigation.** Navigating during descent by comparing camera images with onboard maps, used by Perseverance and SLIM to land precisely (27, 48).

**Thermal vacuum test.** Testing a spacecraft in a chamber that reproduces space vacuum and temperature extremes (4).

**Three-axis stabilisation.** Controlling a spacecraft's attitude actively about all three axes (6).

**Throttling.** Varying a rocket engine's thrust (35).

**Thrust-to-weight ratio.** Thrust divided by weight; it must exceed 1 for a rocket to lift off (34).

**Total electron content (TEC).** The number of free electrons in a column through the ionosphere, which determines GNSS signal delay (18, 23).

**Training data.** The examples from which a machine-learning model learns (43).

**Transfer orbit.** An intermediate orbit between two others, such as a geostationary transfer orbit (3).

**Transformer.** A neural-network architecture based on attention, used in large language models and increasingly for images and weather (44).

**Transit method.** Detecting an exoplanet by the small dip in starlight as it passes in front of its star (39).

**Transponder.** A unit on a communication satellite that receives, amplifies, frequency-shifts and retransmits signals (17).

**Troposphere.** The lowest layer of the atmosphere, up to 8–18 km, where weather occurs (2, 16).

**True anomaly (ν).** The angle from periapsis to a body's current position, measured at the central body (3).

**Turbopump.** A turbine-driven pump that feeds propellant into a rocket engine at high pressure (35).

**Two-line element set (TLE).** A standard text format for publishing the orbits of catalogued objects (3, 21).
:::

## U–Z

::: glossary
**Uncertainty quantification.** Estimating how confident a model's predictions are (44).

**Unsupervised learning.** Machine learning that finds structure in unlabelled data, for example clustering (43).

**Upper stage.** The final stage(s) of a launch vehicle, which deliver the payload to orbit (34).

**Validation / verification.** Verification shows that a product meets its specified requirements ("built it right"); validation shows that it meets the user's real needs ("built the right thing") (4, 53).

**Vernal equinox.** The direction from the Earth to the Sun at the March equinox, used as the reference direction in celestial coordinates (3).

**Vis-viva equation.** The relation between speed, distance and semi-major axis in a two-body orbit (3, B).

**Wavefront sensing and control.** Measuring errors in the light waves reaching a telescope's focus and correcting them by moving mirror segments or deforming mirrors (38).

**Wet mass / dry mass.** A spacecraft's mass with and without propellant (4).

**Whipple shield.** A shield of a thin outer bumper spaced away from the main wall, which breaks up impacting particles (2).

**Window (atmospheric).** A wavelength band in which the atmosphere is relatively transparent (13, D).

**Zenith / nadir.** The point directly overhead / directly below (13).
:::
