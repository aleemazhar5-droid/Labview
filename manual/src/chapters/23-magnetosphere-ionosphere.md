## Geospace

Above about 60 km, the Earth's atmosphere becomes partly ionised by solar radiation. Above about 1 000 km, the environment is dominated by **plasma** — a gas of free electrons and ions. This plasma is controlled by electric and magnetic fields rather than by collisions. This region, from the ionosphere out to the boundary with the solar wind, is called **geospace**. It contains the radiation belts that damage satellites (Chapter 2) and the ionosphere that affects every radio signal to and from space (Chapters 7 and 18). It also contains the thermosphere, whose drag determines satellite lifetimes (Chapters 11 and 21). Understanding its physics is the basis of space weather forecasting (Chapter 22).

## The Geomagnetic Field

### A Tilted Dipole

The Earth's magnetic field is generated by a dynamo in the liquid iron outer core. At the surface it is approximately that of a **magnetic dipole** tilted by about 10° from the rotation axis. Its field strength is about 30 µT at the equator and 60 µT near the poles. In a dipole, the field strength falls with distance as $r^{-3}$, and field lines follow

$$r = L\cos^2\lambda$$

where λ is magnetic latitude and $L$ is the distance, in Earth radii ($R_E$), at which the field line crosses the magnetic equator (@fig:dipole). The **L-shell** parameter is the most convenient way to label regions of the magnetosphere. The inner radiation belt lies at L ≈ 1.2–2, GNSS satellites at L ≈ 4–4.5, and geostationary satellites at L ≈ 6.6.

![Dipole magnetic field lines labelled by L-shell. Trapped particles spiral along these lines; geostationary satellites are on the L = 6.6 shell.](figures/out/ch23_dipole.svg){#fig:dipole width=92%}

### A Changing Field

The field is not static. The north magnetic pole has drifted from northern Canada towards Siberia at up to 50 km per year. The dipole moment has weakened by about 9 % since the mid-19th century. Over geological time the field has reversed many times — most recently about 780 000 years ago. A region of weak field over South America and the South Atlantic, the **South Atlantic Anomaly**, allows the inner radiation belt to dip to altitudes of 200–800 km. LEO satellites — including the ISS and the Hubble Space Telescope — pass through it several times a day. They experience most of their radiation dose and single-event upsets there. The field is monitored by ground observatories and by ESA's three **Swarm** satellites (2013). It is modelled by the International Geomagnetic Reference Field (IGRF) and the World Magnetic Model. These models are updated every five years and used in every smartphone compass and aircraft heading system.

## The Magnetosphere

### Structure

The supersonic solar wind cannot penetrate the Earth's magnetic field. Instead, the field carves out a cavity — the **magnetosphere** — shaped like a comet, compressed on the dayside and stretched into a long tail on the nightside (@fig:magnetosphere):

- The **bow shock**, about 13 $R_E$ upstream, where the solar wind slows abruptly from supersonic to subsonic speed;
- the **magnetosheath** of shocked, turbulent solar-wind plasma;
- the **magnetopause**, the boundary where the pressure of the solar wind balances the magnetic pressure of the Earth's field — typically about 10 $R_E$ from the Earth on the sunward side;
- the polar **cusps**, funnel-shaped regions through which solar-wind plasma can reach the upper atmosphere;
- the **magnetotail**, extending more than 200 $R_E$ downstream, consisting of two **lobes** of nearly empty, open field lines separated by the hot **plasma sheet**;
- the inner magnetosphere: the cold, dense **plasmasphere** (an extension of the ionosphere to about 4–5 $R_E$), the **radiation belts** and the **ring current** of energetic ions that flows around the Earth during storms.

![Structure of the Earth's magnetosphere (schematic, not to scale). The Sun is to the right.](figures/out/ch23_magnetosphere.svg){#fig:magnetosphere width=96%}

### Where Is the Magnetopause?

The distance to the magnetopause follows from pressure balance. The solar wind's dynamic pressure $P = \rho v^2$ must equal the magnetic pressure of the compressed dipole field $B^2/2\mu_0$; allowing for a factor of about 2 compression by the boundary currents, the standoff distance is

$$r_{mp} \approx \left(\frac{2B_0^2}{\mu_0\,P}\right)^{1/6} R_E$$

with $B_0 \approx 31$ µT the equatorial surface field. For a typical pressure of 2 nPa this gives $r_{mp} ≈ 9.6\ R_E$; because of the sixth root, even large pressure increases move it only modestly (@fig:standoff). During extreme storms, high pressure combined with erosion by a southward interplanetary field pushes the magnetopause inside geostationary orbit, exposing GEO satellites directly to the solar wind and the magnetosheath.

![Subsolar magnetopause distance versus solar-wind dynamic pressure, from pressure balance and from an empirical model.](figures/out/ch23_standoff.svg){#fig:standoff width=84%}

## Charged Particles in Magnetic Fields

### Three Periodic Motions

A charged particle in a magnetic field performs three superimposed periodic motions (@fig:motion):

1. **Gyration** around the field line, with the **gyrofrequency** $f_g = qB/(2\pi\gamma m)$ — tens of kilohertz for electrons and a few hertz for protons in the inner magnetosphere.
2. **Bounce** along the field line between **mirror points**. As a particle moves towards stronger field near the poles, its velocity component perpendicular to the field grows at the expense of the parallel component (conserving its magnetic moment) until it reverses direction. Particles whose **pitch angle** is too small mirror below about 100 km altitude, where they are lost by collisions with the atmosphere — they are said to be in the **loss cone**.
3. **Drift** around the Earth, caused by the gradient and curvature of the field: electrons drift eastward and ions westward, forming the ring current.

The three periods differ by orders of magnitude (@fig:periods): gyration in microseconds to fractions of a second, bounce in fractions of a second to seconds, drift in minutes to hours. Each motion is associated with an **adiabatic invariant** that is conserved when the fields change slowly compared with that period. Violating the invariants — through waves, sudden compressions or electric fields — accelerates, transports and removes particles.

![The three periodic motions of trapped particles: gyration, bounce between mirror points and drift around the Earth.](figures/out/ch23_motion.svg){#fig:motion width=96%}

### The Van Allen Radiation Belts

The first US satellite, **Explorer 1** (1958), carried a Geiger counter designed by James Van Allen of the University of Iowa. It recorded puzzling drop-outs — later understood as saturation by intense radiation — revealing that the Earth is surrounded by belts of trapped energetic particles. The **radiation belts** (@fig:radbelts) consist of:

- An **inner belt** at L ≈ 1.2–2, dominated by protons of tens to hundreds of MeV, created mainly by the decay of neutrons produced by cosmic rays hitting the atmosphere (cosmic-ray albedo neutron decay). It is very stable.
- An **outer belt** at L ≈ 3–7, dominated by electrons of hundreds of keV to several MeV, which varies by orders of magnitude within hours during storms, as wave–particle interactions accelerate and scatter electrons.
- A **slot region** at L ≈ 2–3, kept relatively empty by scattering of electrons into the loss cone by plasmaspheric waves.

NASA's twin **Van Allen Probes** (2012–2019) transformed understanding of the belts. Within days of launch they discovered a transient **third belt** (@fig:thirdbelt). They also showed that electrons are accelerated locally by "chorus" waves inside the belts, and found a sharp barrier at about 11 000 km that ultra-relativistic electrons cannot cross. Engineering models of the trapped radiation environment, such as AE8/AP8 and their successors AE9/AP9/SPM, are used to design spacecraft (Chapter 2).

![Schematic radial profiles of trapped protons and electrons in the radiation belts. The outer electron belt varies greatly with geomagnetic activity.](figures/out/ch23_belts.svg){#fig:radbelts width=86%}

![The Van Allen Probes discovered in September 2012 that a third, transient radiation belt (in the middle) can form between the inner and outer belts. Visualisation by NASA.](figures/photos/ch23_belt3.jpg){#fig:thirdbelt width=64%}

The belts can also be created artificially. The US **Starfish Prime** nuclear test in July 1962, at 400 km altitude, injected high-energy electrons that formed an artificial belt lasting years. It damaged or destroyed about a third of the satellites then in orbit — one reason why nuclear explosions in space are now banned (Chapter 19).

## Coupling the Solar Wind to the Magnetosphere

### Magnetic Reconnection

How does solar-wind energy enter a magnetosphere that the solar wind cannot penetrate? The answer, proposed by James Dungey in 1961, is **magnetic reconnection**. When the interplanetary magnetic field points southward — opposite to the Earth's field at the dayside magnetopause — field lines from the two regions can break and reconnect. Terrestrial field lines then become connected to the solar wind (@fig:dungey). These "open" field lines are swept back over the poles by the solar wind and pile up in the tail lobes. There they reconnect again in the plasma sheet, and the closed field lines return to the dayside. This **Dungey cycle** drives a large-scale circulation of plasma — two convection cells in the polar ionosphere — and transfers energy, mass and momentum from the solar wind into the magnetosphere. When the interplanetary field is northward, reconnection occurs at high latitudes instead and the magnetosphere is relatively quiet.

![The Dungey cycle. Reconnection with a southward interplanetary magnetic field opens dayside field lines, which are carried over the poles into the tail, where they reconnect and return.](figures/out/ch23_dungey.svg){#fig:dungey width=94%}

Reconnection converts magnetic energy into particle energy in tiny regions, a few kilometres across for electrons, where the plasma decouples from the magnetic field. NASA's four **Magnetospheric Multiscale (MMS)** spacecraft were launched in 2015 and fly in a tetrahedron only a few kilometres across. They were the first to fly through these electron diffusion regions and measure the physics of reconnection directly (@fig:mms). The same physics also operates in solar flares, fusion reactors and astrophysical plasmas.

![Artist's impression of NASA's four Magnetospheric Multiscale spacecraft flying in formation through a region of magnetic reconnection.](figures/photos/ch23_mms.jpg){#fig:mms width=66%}

### Substorms and Storms

Energy stored in the stretched tail is released intermittently in **substorms**. Over about an hour, the tail field becomes increasingly stretched (growth phase). Then reconnection in the near-Earth tail suddenly releases energy (expansion phase), and the tail field snaps back towards a dipole. Particles are injected into the inner magnetosphere, and the aurora brightens and surges poleward and westward. Substorms occur several times a day even in moderately disturbed conditions; NASA's five **THEMIS** satellites (2007) were designed to determine where and how they start.

A **geomagnetic storm** is a longer, larger disturbance lasting one to several days, driven by a sustained period of strong southward interplanetary field — usually from a CME or a corotating interaction region (Chapter 22). During a storm, ions injected from the tail build up the **ring current**, whose magnetic field depresses the horizontal field at low latitudes (measured by the Dst index). The radiation belts are also reconfigured, and large currents flow along field lines into the polar ionosphere (**field-aligned** or Birkeland currents). These currents close through the auroral electrojets.

## The Aurora

The aurora is produced when electrons with energies of about 1–10 keV, accelerated in the magnetosphere above the ionosphere, plunge along field lines into the upper atmosphere. There they excite oxygen atoms and nitrogen molecules, which emit light as they return to lower energy states:

- **Green** light at 557.7 nm from atomic oxygen, at about 100–250 km — the most common auroral colour;
- **Red** light at 630.0 nm from atomic oxygen, at higher altitudes (above about 200 km), where collisions are rare enough for this slow transition to occur;
- **Blue and violet** light from ionised molecular nitrogen (391.4 and 427.8 nm) at lower altitudes, often seen at the bottom edge of active displays.

Aurorae occur in two **auroral ovals** centred on the geomagnetic poles, typically between about 65° and 75° magnetic latitude. During storms the ovals expand towards the equator (@fig:oval): in the May 2024 storm, aurorae were seen at magnetic latitudes below 30°. Astronauts on the ISS, whose orbit reaches 51.6° latitude, regularly fly through the auroral zones (@fig:auroraiss). Space-based ultraviolet imagers have photographed the entire oval from above since the 1980s.

![The aurora australis above the Indian Ocean, photographed from the International Space Station on 12 June 2025 between Australia and Antarctica.](figures/photos/ch23_aurora.jpg){#fig:auroraiss width=80%}

## The Upper Atmosphere

Above about 90 km, the neutral atmosphere changes character (@fig:atmosphere). In the **thermosphere**, absorption of solar extreme-ultraviolet radiation heats the gas to 600–2 000 K, depending on solar activity. Molecules are dissociated, so **atomic oxygen** becomes the dominant species between about 200 and 600 km. This atomic oxygen causes material erosion on spacecraft (Chapter 2). Above about 600–1 000 km, in the **exosphere**, helium and then hydrogen dominate and atoms move on ballistic paths, sometimes escaping to space. Because the thermosphere expands when heated, its density at a given altitude varies by a factor of ten or more over the solar cycle. Its density also increases several-fold during storms. This is the main reason for uncertainty in predicting satellite orbits (Chapters 21 and 22). NASA's TIMED (2001), GOLD (2018) and ICON (2019–2022) missions have studied how the thermosphere responds both to the Sun from above and to atmospheric waves from below.

![Temperature of the neutral atmosphere with altitude: US Standard Atmosphere below 86 km and a thermospheric profile above, with the range between solar minimum and maximum.](figures/out/ch23_atmosphere.svg){#fig:atmosphere width=80%}

## The Ionosphere

### Formation and Layers

Solar extreme-ultraviolet and X-ray radiation ionises the upper atmosphere, creating the **ionosphere** between about 60 and 1 000 km. Its electron density at each altitude results from a balance between production, which is greatest where the radiation is absorbed (a Chapman layer), and loss by recombination and transport. The ionosphere has distinct layers (@fig:ionosphere):

- the **D region** (60–90 km), present only in daytime, weakly ionised but dense enough in neutral gas to absorb HF radio waves;
- the **E region** (90–150 km), with a peak near 110 km and occasional thin, intense layers of metallic ions ("sporadic E");
- the **F1 layer** (150–220 km), present in daytime;
- the **F2 layer** (220–500 km), the densest, with peak densities of about 10¹¹–10¹² electrons per m³, which persists at night because recombination is slow at high altitudes.

![Electron density profiles of the ionosphere by day and by night (schematic Chapman-layer model).](figures/out/ch23_ionosphere.svg){#fig:ionosphere width=80%}

### Variations and Irregularities

The ionosphere varies with time of day, season, solar cycle (peak densities are several times higher at solar maximum) and geomagnetic activity. Near the magnetic equator, the eastward electric field of the daytime ionosphere lifts plasma upward ("fountain effect"). The plasma then slides down the field lines to form two crests of high density about 15° north and south of the magnetic equator. This is the **equatorial ionization anomaly**, where total electron content reaches its global maximum (@fig:tecmap). After sunset, the bottom of the equatorial F region can become unstable, forming **plasma bubbles** — depletions that rise through the ionosphere and fill with irregularities of all scales, causing strong **scintillation** of satellite signals. At high latitudes, polar-cap patches, auroral precipitation and travelling ionospheric disturbances create other irregularities.

![Schematic global total electron content in the afternoon near solar maximum, showing the crests of the equatorial ionization anomaly.](figures/out/ch23_tecmap.png){#fig:tecmap width=96%}

### Radio Propagation

The ionosphere's effect on radio waves depends on the **plasma frequency**

$$f_p \approx 8.98\sqrt{n_e}\ \text{Hz}$$

where $n_e$ is the electron density in m⁻³. Radio waves below the local plasma frequency cannot propagate and are reflected; waves above it pass through but are delayed and refracted.

- **HF skywave propagation**: waves at 3–30 MHz are reflected by the ionosphere, allowing over-the-horizon communication and broadcasting worldwide. The highest frequency reflected at vertical incidence is the **critical frequency** foF2 (5–15 MHz); at oblique incidence the **maximum usable frequency** is higher by roughly the secant of the angle of incidence.
- **Ionosondes** sweep a radio frequency upward and measure the delay of the echo, producing an **ionogram** of virtual height versus frequency (@fig:ionogram), from which the electron-density profile is reconstructed. Networks of ionosondes, and over-the-horizon radars, rely on the same physics.
- **Satellite signals** at frequencies far above the plasma frequency pass through, but they are delayed. The delay is proportional to the total electron content divided by the frequency squared (40.3 TEC/f² metres; Chapter 18). Their polarisation is also rotated by the magnetic field (**Faraday rotation**), which affects low-frequency radars and radio astronomy.
- **Scintillation**, measured by the S4 index (amplitude) and σφ (phase), disrupts GNSS and satellite communications at L-band and below, mainly near the equator after sunset and at high latitudes.

![A model electron-density profile (left) and the ionogram computed from it (right). Virtual heights rise steeply near the critical frequencies of the E and F2 layers, where the signal is slowed most.](figures/out/ch23_ionogram.svg){#fig:ionogram width=98%}

## Measuring and Modelling Geospace

### Observations

Geospace is studied with a combination of ground and space instruments:

- **Ground**: magnetometer networks (which provide the Kp, Dst and AE indices), ionosondes, and coherent-scatter radars (SuperDARN, which maps ionospheric convection over both polar regions). There are also **incoherent-scatter radars** (such as those at Millstone Hill, Jicamarca and the European EISCAT system, being replaced by the volumetric EISCAT_3D). Other instruments are GNSS receiver networks that map total electron content globally, all-sky cameras and riometers.
- **Space**: missions from Explorer 1 onward (@fig:geomissions). They include ISEE, Dynamics Explorer, Geotail, Polar and Wind, ESA's four-spacecraft **Cluster** (2000–2024), THEMIS, the Van Allen Probes, Swarm, MMS, Japan's Arase (2016), GOLD and ICON. Radio occultation also provides ionospheric profiles (Chapter 16). NASA's **TRACERS** twin satellites (launched July 2025) study reconnection in the cusp. The ESA–Chinese Academy of Sciences mission **SMILE** was launched on 19 May 2026. It carries a soft X-ray imager that, for the first time, images the magnetopause and cusps from outside, together with an ultraviolet auroral imager.

### Other Planets

Magnetospheres are common in the Solar System (@tbl:planetmag). Jupiter's is the largest structure in the Solar System after the heliosphere — its magnetopause lies 50–100 Jovian radii from the planet. Its intense radiation belts, fed by sulphur and oxygen from Io's volcanoes, constrain the design of every Jupiter mission (Juno, JUICE, Europa Clipper). Mercury's small magnetosphere is so compressed that the solar wind sometimes reaches its surface. Mars and Venus have no global fields, so the solar wind interacts directly with their upper atmospheres and strips them away, as measured by MAVEN.

| Planet | Global field (relative to Earth's dipole moment) | Typical magnetopause distance | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mercury | ≈ 0.0006 | ≈ 1.5 planetary radii | Studied by MESSENGER; BepiColombo arrives in 2026 |
| Venus | None (induced magnetosphere) | — | Ionosphere interacts directly with the solar wind |

::: example
**Example: HF communication across the Atlantic**

An airline crew over the North Atlantic must contact a ground station 3 000 km away by HF radio. At night the F2-layer peak is at about 300 km with foF2 = 5 MHz.

1. For a single-hop path of 3 000 km, the ray reflects at the midpoint, 1 500 km from each end. Allowing for the Earth's curvature, the elevation angle at the ground is only a few degrees and the angle of incidence on the layer is about 75°, so the secant factor is about 3.5–4.
2. The maximum usable frequency is therefore about 5 × 3.7 ≈ 18 MHz. Operators choose a working frequency about 15 % below this, around 15 MHz.
3. After sunrise, foF2 rises to about 10 MHz and the MUF to more than 30 MHz, but the D region also forms and absorbs lower frequencies, so the crew must move to a higher band.
4. During a polar-cap absorption event after a solar proton event (Chapter 22), D-region absorption at high latitudes can block HF entirely for days; aircraft then use satellite communications or reroute to lower latitudes.
:::

::: ai
**AI in magnetospheric and ionospheric physics**

Neural networks trained on decades of satellite and ground data now predict geomagnetic indices, map radiation-belt electron fluxes, forecast ionospheric total electron content and scintillation, and reconstruct three-dimensional ionospheric structure from sparse GNSS observations. Physics-informed machine learning combines these data-driven models with the conservation laws of plasma physics (Chapters 44 and 47).

::: key
**Key takeaways**

- The Earth's field is approximately a tilted dipole; field lines are labelled by L-shell. The South Atlantic Anomaly brings the inner belt down to LEO altitudes.
- The magnetosphere is bounded by the magnetopause (about 10 R_E sunward) and bow shock and has a long tail; the standoff distance follows from pressure balance and scales as P^(−1/6).
- Trapped particles gyrate, bounce and drift; the radiation belts comprise a stable inner proton belt and a highly variable outer electron belt separated by a slot.
- Magnetic reconnection with a southward interplanetary field drives the Dungey cycle, substorms and storms; MMS measured reconnection directly.
- The aurora is produced by 1–10 keV electrons exciting oxygen (green, red) and nitrogen (blue, violet) in the auroral ovals, which expand during storms.
- The ionosphere's D, E, F1 and F2 layers reflect HF radio below the plasma frequency (f_p ≈ 9√n_e Hz) and delay and scintillate satellite signals.
- Geospace is monitored by ground networks and missions from Explorer 1 to SMILE (2026), and described by standard models used throughout space engineering.
