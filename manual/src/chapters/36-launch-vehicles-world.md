Between October 1957 and October 2026 the world made about 7 400 orbital launch attempts. For most of that time the annual total stayed between 50 and 140; then, after 2018, it took off. In 2025 there were 330 attempts, of which 318 succeeded, and by 13 October 2026 another 244 had been made. Half of all launches in 2025 used a single type of rocket, SpaceX's Falcon 9, and most of those carried the company's own Starlink satellites. At the same time more countries and companies than ever were building rockets: China alone flew sixteen different families of launch vehicles in 2025.

This chapter is a field guide to that fleet. It does not repeat the engineering of rockets — the rocket equation, staging, structures and guidance are the subject of Chapter 34, and engines of Chapter 35 — but uses it to compare vehicles: how much they lift, what they burn, whether they can be reused, how often they fly and fail, and what they cost. Launch sites and ground systems are covered in Chapter 37, and suborbital rockets in Chapter 9. The launch statistics here come from Jonathan McDowell's General Catalog of Artificial Space Objects (GCAT) as of 13 October 2026; Appendix E lists every launch of 2025 and 2026 and the all-time totals by country, vehicle family and launch site.

## A Short History of the Launch Vehicle

### Missiles Become Launchers

The first launch vehicles were ballistic missiles with a satellite in place of a warhead. Sergei Korolev's **R-7** was built to throw a nuclear warhead of about 5 t across continents; it had a central core and four strap-on boosters, all burning kerosene and liquid oxygen and all ignited on the ground. A modified R-7 launched Sputnik on 4 October 1957, a version with an upper stage carried Yuri Gagarin in 1961, and its descendants — Vostok, Voskhod, Molniya and Soyuz — have flown about 1 970 times, more than any other rocket family (@fig:lwtimeline). Crews still fly to the International Space Station on a Soyuz-2, a modernised R-7 with digital avionics.

The United States followed a different route. The civilian Vanguard exploded on its first orbital attempt in December 1957, and the Army's Juno I, derived from the Redstone missile, put Explorer 1 into orbit on 31 January 1958. Three military missiles then became the American workhorses for half a century. The **Thor** intermediate-range missile grew, stage by stage, into the **Delta** family; the last Delta II flew in 2018. The **Atlas** missile, with its balloon tanks held rigid by internal pressure, launched the orbital Mercury flights and, topped by the hydrogen-fuelled **Centaur** stage (first successful flight in 1963), the Surveyor Moon landers and many planetary probes. The **Titan II** missile carried the Gemini crews, and with large solid boosters became the Titan III and IV, which launched heavy military satellites until 2005.

### The Moon Race and the Super-Heavies

The race to the Moon produced the largest rockets of the century. NASA's **Saturn V** stood 110.6 m tall and weighed almost 3 000 t at lift-off; its five F-1 engines (Chapter 35) gave 34 MN of thrust, and the three stages could place about 140 t in low Earth orbit (LEO), including the third stage and the Apollo spacecraft bound for the Moon. All thirteen Saturn Vs launched between 1967 and 1973 reached space (@fig:lwsaturnv); the last lifted the Skylab station. The Soviet answer, the **N1**, had thirty engines in its first stage. Its first stage was never fired on a test stand as a complete stage, and all four launches between 1969 and 1972 failed in that stage — through engine fires, a turbopump explosion and control problems — before the programme was cancelled. The next Soviet super-heavy, **Energia**, flew only twice, in 1987 and 1988, the second time carrying the uncrewed Buran shuttle; its four strap-on boosters were essentially first stages of the Zenit rocket.

### Shuttles and Workhorses

The **Space Shuttle** (Chapter 10) promised cheap, routine access by reusing its orbiter and solid boosters. It flew 135 missions between 1981 and 2011 and carried most of the International Space Station into orbit (@fig:lwshuttle), but refurbishment was slow and costly, and two orbiters were lost with their crews, *Challenger* in 1986 and *Columbia* in 2003. After *Challenger* the United States stopped launching commercial satellites on the Shuttle; the business moved back to expendable rockets, revived the Delta, Atlas and Titan lines and gave Europe's new Ariane its opening.

In the Soviet Union, two families did most of the work. The **Proton**, first launched in 1965, burns storable nitrogen tetroxide and unsymmetrical dimethylhydrazine (UDMH) — toxic but able to sit in the tanks for weeks — and lifted the Salyut and Mir stations, the Russian modules of the ISS and many geostationary satellites; about 430 have flown. The **Soyuz-U** flew 787 times between 1973 and 2017 (counting its PVB variant), at up to 47 launches a year. In the early 1980s the USSR launched about 100 rockets a year — 108 in 1982 — mostly carrying short-lived reconnaissance satellites that returned their film to Earth.

### New Launching Nations

France became the third nation to launch its own satellite, with the Diamant rocket in 1965. Japan followed in February 1970 with the small solid Lambda-4S, China in April 1970 with the Long March 1, the United Kingdom in 1971 with Black Arrow and India in 1980 with the SLV-3. Israel (1988), Iran (2009), North Korea (2012) and South Korea (2013, with a Russian first stage; 2022 with its own Nuri) joined later. @tbl:lwhistory lists the landmark vehicles.

After the failure of the multinational Europa rocket, Europe built **Ariane 1**, first launched on 24 December 1979. Its successor Ariane 4 (1988–2003) took about half of the commercial geostationary market, and **Ariane 5** (1996–2023) flew 117 times, carrying two large satellites at once to geostationary transfer orbit (GTO) and, in its most famous flight, the James Webb Space Telescope (@fig:lwariane5). China's Long March family grew from the Long March 1 through the Long March 2 (which returned film capsules and, as the 2F, has launched every Shenzhou spacecraft since 1999 and every crew since 2003) to the hydrogen-fuelled Long March 3 for geostationary missions. Japan developed both a solid-rocket line for science (Mu, M-V and Epsilon) and liquid rockets, first built under licence from the Delta, then the all-Japanese **H-II** (1994) and **H-IIA**, which flew 50 times from 2001 to 2025 with a single failure (@fig:lwh2a). India's four-stage **PSLV** (1993) launched Chandrayaan-1 and the Mars Orbiter Mission, and its LVM3 (originally GSLV Mk III) carried Chandrayaan-2 and -3.

The balance of power shifted twice (@fig:lwcountries). Until the mid-1960s the United States made most launches; from 1970 to the end of the 1980s the Soviet Union made 70–85 % of them; after its collapse the totals fell and Europe, China, Japan and India took a growing share. In 2025 the United States and China made nearly nine launches in ten. Diversity has grown too: in 2026 Chinese providers flew 19 different vehicle families and American providers about a dozen, against two or three in Russia, Europe and Japan.

### United States

**SpaceX** dominates the world's launch market. Falcon 9 made 165 of the 330 attempts in 2025 and 113 more by mid-October 2026. Its first stage lands on a droneship or a landing zone after almost every flight (Chapter 34): the fleet leader flew for the 37th time in August 2026. SpaceX lists a Falcon 9 at $74 million in 2026. **Falcon Heavy**, three Falcon 9 cores strapped together, did not fly in 2025 but launched three times in 2026, including NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope on 30 August. **Starship**, designed for full reuse, is the largest rocket ever built: the Version 3 vehicle stands 124.4 m tall, weighs about 5 000 t and has 33 Raptor engines on its Super Heavy booster and six on the ship. After three ships were lost in the first half of 2025, the last two flights of Version 2 succeeded, and on Flight 14, on 28 September 2026, a ship reached orbit for the first time, deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites and returned to splash down in the Pacific three hours later. Catching the ship on the launch tower, the step needed for full reuse, was the next goal, expected on Flight 15, and NASA's Artemis lunar landings depend on refuelling Starships in orbit, which has yet to be demonstrated.

NASA's **Space Launch System** (SLS) is the government's own super-heavy rocket. It uses Space Shuttle engines and five-segment solid boosters and is thrown away on every flight. After the uncrewed Artemis I in 2022 it launched the four astronauts of Artemis II around the Moon on 1 April 2026 (@fig:lwsls). In February 2026 NASA dropped plans for the larger Exploration Upper Stage, and in March it chose a Centaur V upper stage for later SLS rockets; NASA's Inspector General has estimated that each Artemis launch costs more than $4 billion including the Orion spacecraft and ground systems.

![The SLS rocket lifts off from Pad 39B with the four Artemis II astronauts aboard Orion on 1 April 2026. The two solid boosters provide more than three-quarters of the lift-off thrust.](figures/photos/ch36_sls.jpg){#fig:lwsls width=64%}

**United Launch Alliance** (ULA), owned equally by Boeing and Lockheed Martin, is replacing its Atlas V and the retired Delta IV with **Vulcan Centaur**, whose first stage burns liquefied natural gas in two Blue Origin BE-4 engines. Atlas V flew 110 times from 2002 without losing a payload; its last Amazon Leo launch, on 2 July 2026, left six rockets reserved for Boeing's Starliner capsule. Vulcan has had a difficult start: on its second flight in October 2024 the nozzle fell off one of its solid boosters, and when a booster anomaly recurred on the USSF-87 mission in February 2026 the Space Force paused national-security launches on Vulcan. ULA had planned 16–18 Vulcan launches in 2026; by mid-October it had made one, and return to flight was reported for late October.

**Blue Origin's New Glenn** is a 98 m, 7 m diameter rocket with a reusable first stage powered by seven BE-4 engines. It reached orbit on its first flight in January 2025, landed its booster on a ship on its second, which launched NASA's ESCAPADE Mars probes in November 2025 (@fig:lwnewglenn), and re-flew that booster in April 2026 — but the upper stage under-performed and left AST SpaceMobile's BlueBird 7 satellite in a useless orbit. On 28 May 2026 a New Glenn exploded during a hot-fire test before its fourth flight, wrecking the transporter-erector at Launch Complex 36. Blue Origin traced the cause to an oxygen valve on one BE-4 and aimed to fly again before the end of 2026.

Smaller American rockets serve satellites below about a tonne. **Rocket Lab's Electron**, an 18 m carbon-composite rocket with electric-pump-fed engines (@fig:lwelectron), has made 88 orbital launches with four failures and flew 18 times in 2025, more than any other single rocket type except Falcon 9; its average revenue per launch was $9.1 million in mid-2026. Rocket Lab's reusable medium-lift **Neutron** slipped to 2027 after a tank failed in testing. **Firefly Alpha** returned to flight in March 2026 after a failure in April 2025 and the loss of a booster in a ground test; Firefly and Northrop Grumman are developing the larger Eclipse for 2027. Northrop Grumman's solid **Minotaur IV** flew once in each of 2025 and 2026, and the air-launched **Pegasus XL** — dropped from an L-1011 airliner — launched in July 2026 a robotic servicer intended to raise the orbit of NASA's ageing Swift observatory. Relativity's Terran R and Stoke Space's Nova had not flown by October 2026.

### Europe

**Ariane 6** replaced Ariane 5 in July 2024. It keeps a hydrogen-fuelled core stage with a Vulcain 2.1 engine, adds a restartable Vinci upper stage (Chapter 35) and carries either two (Ariane 62) or four (Ariane 64) P120C solid boosters. By October 2026 it had made nine flights: the first in 2024, four Ariane 62s in 2025, then the first Ariane 64 on 12 February 2026, which placed 32 Amazon Leo satellites in orbit. In June 2026 the first Ariane 64 with the larger P160C boosters carried 36 of them, and Amazon raised its order from 18 to 24 Ariane 64 launches. **Vega C**, a four-stage rocket whose solid first stage is the same P120C motor used as Ariane 6's booster, returned from a 2022 failure in December 2024 and is now marketed by its builder Avio; it launched the European–Chinese SMILE mission in May 2026.

Europe's dependence on so few rockets became painful in 2023–24, when Ariane 5 retired, Vega C was grounded and Soyuz launches from Kourou ended after Russia's invasion of Ukraine; ESA had to buy Falcon 9 launches for Euclid, Galileo satellites and Hera. One response is the **European Launcher Challenge**: ESA's member states committed €902 million at their November 2025 ministerial meeting, and in August 2026 ESA signed conditional contracts with Isar Aerospace (€197.8 million), Rocket Factory Augsburg (€186.9 million) and PLD Space (€158.9 million), each required to reach orbit by the end of 2027. Isar's **Spectrum** failed 30 s into its first flight in March 2025 but reached orbit from Andøya, Norway, on 5 September 2026 — the first satellite delivery by a European commercial launch company. RFA One and PLD's Miura 5 were preparing for first flights, MaiaSpace was developing a partly reusable rocket, and Orbex had closed down.

### Russia

Russia launched only 17 rockets in 2025 and 13 by mid-October 2026. The workhorse is **Soyuz-2** (@fig:lwsoyuz), which launches crews and Progress cargo ships to the ISS from Baikonur in Kazakhstan, military satellites from Plesetsk and other missions from Vostochny in the Far East. When the Soyuz MS-28 crew launched in November 2025, a service platform fell into the flame trench of Site 31, Russia's only pad for crewed flights; it was repaired by March 2026. The modular **Angara** family, built around a common kerosene core, is meant to replace Proton and give Russia heavy launches from its own territory, but has flown only a dozen times since 2014. **Proton-M** made one launch in 2026. The new **Soyuz-5** medium rocket, with a single RD-171MV engine on its first stage, made a suborbital test flight from Baikonur on 30 April 2026.

### China

China launched 93 rockets in 2025 and 71 by mid-October 2026, more than half of them Long Marches built by the state-owned China Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation (CASC). The older Long March 2, 3 and 4 burn storable N₂O₄/UDMH; a new generation burns kerosene and oxygen (Long March 6, 7, 8 and 12) or hydrogen (the Long March 5 heavy-lift rocket and upper stages). Launches increasingly carry China's two broadband mega-constellations, Guowang and Qianfan, from the new commercial launch site next to Wenchang on Hainan Island. For crewed lunar missions CASC is developing the **Long March 10**: a 92.5 m, three-core rocket with 21 kerosene engines, able to send 27 t towards the Moon, due to fly in 2027. A single-core version, the Long March 10A, made a suborbital test with an abort of the Mengzhou crew capsule in February 2026, and the commercial **Long March 10B** reached orbit on its first flight on 10 July 2026, when its first stage was caught at sea by hooks snagging cables stretched over a ship — China's first recovery of an orbital booster.

Since 2015 Chinese policy has opened launch to private and semi-private companies, and their rockets made about a quarter of China's launches in 2025. LandSpace's **Zhuque-2** became the first methane-fuelled rocket to reach orbit in 2023; its stainless-steel **Zhuque-3** reached orbit in December 2025 and in August 2026 landed its first stage on legs at a desert site in Gansu province, although a fire then broke out at its base. Galactic Energy's solid **Ceres-1** has flown 23 times, but its liquid Ceres-2 failed on its debut in January 2026 before the company's larger **Pallas-1** succeeded in September. CAS Space flies the solid **Kinetica-1** and debuted the 12 t **Kinetica-2** in March 2026, Orienspace launches the solid **Gravity-1** from a ship at sea, and Space Pioneer's **Tianlong-3**, designed as a Falcon 9 rival, failed half a minute after lift-off on its first flight in April 2026. CASC's own reusable test vehicle, the Long March 12A, reached orbit in December 2025 but its booster crashed near the landing pad.

### Japan, India and Other Nations

**Japan** relies on **H3**, a two-stage hydrogen rocket with the expander-bleed LE-9 engine. After a failed first flight in 2023 it succeeded five times, but on its seventh launch (flight F8) in December 2025 the QZS-5 navigation satellite was lost. JAXA's investigation concluded that the most likely cause was bonded joints in the honeycomb satellite adapter that had come apart during manufacture: the adapter buckled and broke piping on the second-stage hydrogen tank. H3 returned to flight in June 2026 with the first H3-30, which has no solid boosters, launched the QZS-7 satellite in August and was scheduled to launch the Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) mission on 20 October 2026. The H-IIA retired in June 2025, the small solid Epsilon S has been delayed by two second-stage motor explosions in ground tests and is now planned with the older Epsilon second stage by March 2027, and the private **Kairos** of Space One failed on all three of its attempts, most recently in March 2026.

**India** suffered two consecutive failures of the PSLV, in May 2025 and January 2026, both in its solid third stage, and had not flown again by October 2026. The **GSLV Mk II** launched the NASA–ISRO NISAR radar satellite in July 2025, and the **LVM3** — renamed HLVM3 in its human-rated form — is being prepared for the first uncrewed Gaganyaan flight, expected at the end of 2026 or in 2027. The small SSLV has flown three times. On 18 July 2026 Skyroot Aerospace's **Vikram-1**, a three-stage solid rocket with a liquid kick stage, reached orbit on its first attempt, the first private Indian rocket to do so.

**South Korea's** three-stage kerosene **Nuri** made its fifth flight on 7 October 2026, delivering five satellites and nine CubeSats — its fourth success in five attempts — while the private Innospace's Hanbit-Nano failed shortly after lift-off from Brazil in December 2025. **Israel** launches its Ofeq reconnaissance satellites westward over the Mediterranean on the solid **Shavit-2**, accepting a retrograde orbit to keep spent stages away from populated land; the latest flew in September 2025. **Iran** launched satellites on the Simorgh and the solid Qaem-100 in 2024, but its Zoljanah failed in September 2025, and **North Korea** has not attempted an orbital launch since a failure in May 2024. **Australia's** first orbital rocket, Gilmour Space's hybrid-propellant Eris, failed 14 s after lift-off in July 2025; a second attempt was expected at the end of 2026. @tbl:lwsmall summarises the small launchers.

## Launch by the Numbers

### Mass to Orbit

Launch counts say little about what is lifted. @fig:lwmass sums the payload mass of every successful launch. The Moon race lifted the world total to almost 500 t a year; from 1981 to 2011 the Space Shuttle added several hundred tonnes a year, although GCAT counts each orbiter — about 100 t that came back to Earth — as payload. Real growth began after 2018. In 2025 launches delivered about 2 870 t of satellites, cargo and crew vehicles, plus 360 t of Starship test ships; including payloads lost in failures, the GCAT total is about 3 500 t, nearly nine times that of 2015. Falcon 9 carried 2 330 t of the 2 870 t — 81 % — and Starlink satellites alone 1 960 t. China lifted 330 t, about 11 %.

### Cadence

The most striking change of the 2020s is cadence — how often a rocket flies (@fig:lwcadence). Rockets used to need weeks or months between flights from one pad, because each was assembled and checked for weeks before launch. Falcon 9 flew 165 times in 2025, a median of 2.0 days between launches. The Soviet Soyuz-U had flown almost as often in the 1970s — it reached 500 launches in 14 years, Falcon 9 in 15 — but only by building a new rocket every time. Falcon 9 reached its 100th launch in late 2020, ten years after its first, and its 600th early in 2026, with most flights on re-used boosters.

## Trends Reshaping Launch

### Reusability

Before December 2015 no orbital booster had ever landed; by 2026 Falcon boosters had landed more than 650 times and three other organisations had recovered orbital first stages: Blue Origin (on a ship), CASC (caught by cables at sea) and LandSpace (on legs, though a fire broke out after landing). Others are following — Rocket Lab's Neutron, Europe's Themis demonstrator, Japan's and India's experimental vehicles — and SpaceX aims to recover both stages of Starship. The economics are discussed in Chapter 34: reuse pays only at a high flight rate, which is why the leaders are operators with their own constellations to launch.

### Rideshare

A **rideshare** mission carries dozens of small satellites from many customers to a common orbit. India's PSLV made it famous by launching 104 satellites at once in 2017; SpaceX's **Transporter** missions to sun-synchronous orbit (Transporter-1 carried 143 in January 2021; Transporter-18 flew on 1 October 2026) and **Bandwagon** missions to mid-inclination orbits now dominate, at a published price of $350 000 for 50 kg. Rideshare undercuts dedicated small launchers on price; its drawbacks are that the satellite goes where and when the main mission goes, which **orbital transfer vehicles** (Chapter 11) partly overcome.

### The Heavy-Lift Race

Between Energia's last flight in 1988 and Falcon Heavy's first in 2018, no rocket able to place more than 30 t in orbit flew. Now four are competing (@fig:lwcapability). Falcon Heavy (64 t expendable) and New Glenn (45 t with a reusable booster) are in service; SLS (95 t) is expensive and has flown only twice, in 2022 and 2026; Starship aims at 100 t or more with full reuse and orbital refuelling. China's Long March 10 (70 t to LEO, 27 t towards the Moon) is due in 2027 to support crewed lunar landings planned by 2030, and Russia has proposed a heavy Angara A5V. Heavy-lift rockets matter for crewed exploration, space stations and large telescopes, but much of the demand that pays for them is the launch of constellation satellites in large batches.

### The Small-Launcher Shake-Out

Around 2018 more than a hundred small-launcher projects were under way worldwide. Few survived: rideshare took much of their market, and the cost of a dedicated small launch per kilogram is high (see the example below). Survivors offer what rideshare cannot — a specific orbit at a specific time, for constellation replenishment, defence "responsive launch" or sovereign needs — and many are growing into medium rockets: Rocket Lab is moving to Neutron, Galactic Energy from Ceres-1 to Pallas-1 and Firefly to Eclipse.

### Sovereign Access

Governments value independent access to space as a matter of security. Europe's crisis of 2023–24, Russia's loss of Western customers after 2022, India's PSLV failures and Japan's H3 failure all show how fragile a fleet of one or two rockets is. The policy response is to fund several competing launchers — the European Launcher Challenge, Japan's support for private rockets, India's opening to Skyroot and others, and the US Space Force's practice of buying launches from several providers — and to build new spaceports in Norway, Sweden, the United Kingdom and Australia (Chapter 37).

::: example
**Example: price per kilogram and payload fraction**

Compare three vehicles at their published prices, assuming each flies full to LEO. **Payload fraction** is payload mass divided by lift-off mass.

1. **Falcon 9** with booster landing: $74 M for 17.5 t → 74 × 10⁶ / 17 500 ≈ **$4 230/kg**. Lift-off mass 549 t → payload fraction 17.5/549 = **3.2 %**. Expending the booster raises the payload to 22.8 t (4.2 %), but the booster is then lost.
2. **Ariane 64**: estimated €115 M for 21.65 t → **€5 310/kg**, about $6 100/kg at an assumed $1.15 per euro. Lift-off mass 860 t → **2.5 %**. The hydrogen core gives high specific impulse, but the four solid boosters add mass.
3. **Electron**: $9.1 M (average revenue per launch, 2026) for 0.3 t → **$30 300/kg**; lift-off mass 13 t → **2.3 %**. Small rockets cost more per kilogram because avionics, range services and launch crews cost almost as much for a small rocket as for a big one.
4. Real missions are rarely full. A 150 kg satellite flying alone on Electron pays about $60 000/kg, while large batches of constellation satellites fill a rocket almost to capacity. The comparison explains why customers who can accept a shared ride choose rideshare at $7 000/kg, and why a dedicated small launch is bought for schedule and orbit, not price.

::: example
**Example: launching a 3 000-satellite constellation**

A broadband operator must deploy 3 000 satellites of about 600 kg — 1 800 t in all. How many launches are needed, and what is the risk (@fig:lwconstellation)?

1. Satellites per launch are limited by the fairing volume and dispensers as much as by mass. Amazon Leo's missions carried 24 satellites on Falcon 9, 27–29 on Atlas V and 32–36 on Ariane 64; New Glenn was to carry 48.
2. Launches needed = ⌈3 000 / *k*⌉: **125** on Falcon 9, **104** on Atlas V, **84** on Ariane 64, **63** on New Glenn, and about **50** on a Starship carrying 60.
3. At four launches a month, the Falcon 9 campaign takes 31 months, the New Glenn campaign 16 months. Real campaigns use several vehicles at once, because no single provider has enough capacity.
4. Risk: with independent launches each failing with probability *p*, the chance of at least one failure is 1 − (1 − *p*)ⁿ. For *p* = 1 % and 125 launches it is 1 − 0.99¹²⁵ = **72 %**; for 63 launches on a new rocket with *p* = 2 %, 1 − 0.98⁶³ = **72 %** as well.
5. The expected number of satellites lost is *N p* — 30 at *p* = 1 %, whichever rocket is used. Bigger rockets reduce the number of launches but put more satellites at risk on each, so operators spread a constellation across several vehicles and keep spares in orbit.

::: ai
**AI in launch operations and range safety**

Machine-learning models are being developed to forecast the weather that most often delays launches — lightning, upper-level winds and anvil clouds — and to help evaluate launch-commit criteria; others scan thousands of telemetry channels during countdowns for anomalies that a human console operator might miss. Range safety, once a human officer with a destruct button, is now largely automated: autonomous flight-safety systems on Falcon 9, Electron and many new rockets decide on board whether to terminate a flight. With hundreds of launches a year, optimisation algorithms schedule pads, ranges, airspace closures and recovery ships, and estimate the risk that falling stages or debris pose to people and aircraft. Because these decisions affect public safety, the algorithms on board stay deterministic and verifiable, while learning-based tools support the humans who certify them; Chapter 52 discusses AI across launch and its assurance.
:::

::: key
**Key takeaways**

- About 7 400 orbital launches have been attempted since 1957; the record year, 2025, saw 330 attempts and 11 failures, 60 % by US and 28 % by Chinese providers.
- Most early launchers were converted missiles: the R-7 (Soyuz) family has flown about 1 970 times and still carries crews. Saturn V, N1, Energia and the Shuttle were the super-heavies of the twentieth century.
- Falcon 9 dominates: half of all launches and 81 % of the satellite mass delivered in 2025, with a median of two days between flights and routinely re-used boosters.
- China flies the most varied fleet — 16–19 vehicle families a year — and in 2026 recovered orbital boosters for the first time; its Long March 10 is due in 2027.
- Europe (Ariane 6, Vega C, Spectrum), Japan (H3), India (LVM3, Vikram-1) and Russia (Soyuz-2, Angara) maintain sovereign access, but setbacks in 2025–26 show how fragile small fleets are.
- Small launchers fail far more often than larger ones (15 % since 2015) and many have disappeared; dedicated small launches cost several times more per kilogram than rideshare.
- Reusability, rideshare, high cadence and a new generation of super-heavies (Starship, SLS, Long March 10) are the trends shaping launch in the late 2020s.
